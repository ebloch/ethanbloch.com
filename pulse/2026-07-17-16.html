Fintech's Charter Bottleneck Is Starting to Loosen

Fintech Takes

Alex Johnson's refreshed Fintech 101 taxonomy is useful because it gets past the lazy question of whether something is or is not fintech and asks where the profit pools and structural constraints actually sit. His sharpest current-market point: modern BaaS was built on artificial charter scarcity. If bank formation keeps opening up, the best fintech programs can graduate into their own charters right when partner banks expected them to become profitable customers.

That connects directly to Hiro's world. The wedge is not just better software; it is knowing which regulated balance-sheet or infrastructure dependency becomes strategic leverage versus rented plumbing. Johnson's adjacent point on regulator competition also matters: charters are not neutral wrappers, they are products sold by agencies with incentives.

My take: if agentic finance becomes real, the regulatory interface will matter as much as the AI interface. The winner will not just answer questions well; it will know which rails, duties, and supervisory expectations attach to each action.

"Charters are the product. Regulators are the vendors. Once you see it, you can't unsee it."

Why it matters: Highly relevant to Hiro: product strategy in consumer finance has to account for regulatory rails, BaaS fragility, and who owns the customer relationship when money movement becomes agent-mediated.

Kimi K3 Shows Why Cute Benchmarks Are Breaking

Simon Willison's Newsletter

Simon Willison tested Moonshot's new Kimi K3, a 2.8T-parameter model billed as the first open 3T-class model, with his long-running pelican SVG benchmark. The model is strong on reported long-horizon knowledge work and frontend coding, but the pelican run exposed practical wrinkles: high reasoning-token burn, expensive output, possible hidden prompt overhead, and a benchmark that no longer tracks the capabilities people actually care about.

The interesting part is not whether Kimi draws a good bird. It is Simon's conclusion that modern model evaluation needs to measure agentic tool use and reliability over long conversations. That is exactly where consumer financial agents will either become trusted operators or expensive demos.

My take: for Hiro, single-turn intelligence is now table stakes. The eval frontier is persistent context, tool reliability, cost control, and graceful recovery when real-world workflows stretch over days.

"The biggest limitation of the pelican is that it doesn’t touch at all on the thing that matters most for today’s model: agentic tool calling and the ability to operate tools reliably as conversations grow in length."

Why it matters: Useful for thinking about Hiro's evaluation stack: benchmark workflows, not model vibes, and measure cost plus reliability under long-running financial tasks.

A Lobotomy History That Reads Like a Warning About Hype

Astral Codex Ten

ACX's book-review finalist on Elliot Valenstein's Great and Desperate Cures is long, grim, and weirdly relevant to any frontier technology that touches human welfare. The review argues that lobotomy spread not because medicine was full of obvious villains, but because desperation, status incentives, weak evidence, media enthusiasm, turf wars, and institutional economics all lined up behind an intervention whose mechanism was poorly understood.

The most unsettling point is that lobotomy did not really end through a clean moral reckoning; it was outcompeted by chlorpromazine, a cheaper and less permanent substitute. Guardrails matter, but so do substitutes. Bad practices often die when a better product changes the economic gradient.

My take: this is the kind of history AI builders should keep close. The danger pattern is not 'evil people do evil things'; it is ambitious people using weak evidence to serve desperate demand before institutions know how to say no.

"Someone would have done it. The demand was too great and the safeguards were too few."

Why it matters: A useful mental model for high-stakes AI products, especially finance and health-adjacent advice: evidence, incentives, and institutional pressure matter as much as technical capability.
