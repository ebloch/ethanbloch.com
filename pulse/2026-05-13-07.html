AI Deployment Starts Looking More Like 1970s Enterprise Computing

Stratechery

OpenAI's new Deployment Company and Google's forward-deployed engineer push are a strong signal that enterprise AI will not be won by chat widgets alone. The labs are moving toward teams that go inside companies, rebuild processes, clean up data, and make AI labor actually replace or reshape work.

Ben Thompson's point is that the better historical analogy is not SaaS or the Internet, but mainframes: top-down executive adoption, expensive implementation, and a buyer focused on bottom-line results. That matters because it explains why model companies suddenly need consulting-style delivery muscle even as they sell automation.

My take: this is directly relevant to Hiro/OpenAI personal finance. The hard part is not just model capability; it is deployment into messy real workflows with permissions, data quality, trust, and exception handling. The winning product surface may be much closer to an operating model than a prettier assistant UI.

"Agents aren't copilots; they are replacements."

Why it matters: Reinforces the thesis that useful AI products need deep workflow integration, not just better prompts.

Headless SaaS Moves the Moat Below the Interface

a16z

a16z uses Salesforce's headless push to ask what survives when agents stop using human-facing software interfaces. The old SaaS moat was habit, dashboards, and humans entering data. In an agentic world, the defensible layers become data models, permissions, workflow logic, compliance, proprietary data generation, networks, and real-world execution.

The strongest line is that AI makes recreating the first 80% of a system of record easier, but the remaining 20% - exceptions, approvals, compliance requirements, edge-case workflows - still separates a wedge from a replacement. That is the same shape as most useful agent products: demos attack the obvious surface area; real businesses live in the exception path.

My take: this is the clearest builder framework in today's sources. For finance, the system of record is not just account data. It is consent, identity, auditability, tax/regulatory context, transaction history, household goals, and permissioned action.

"The defensible data is not the data you import; it's the data your product uniquely causes to exist."

Why it matters: Useful lens for evaluating what parts of personal finance software become agent-readable commodities and what remains durable.

Fine-Tuning Becomes a Specialist Tool, Not the Default Path

AINews / Latent Space

AINews frames OpenAI's fine-tuning API deprecation as part of a broader shift away from fine-tuning as the standard AI engineering move. The claim is not that fine-tuning is dead; rather, the median product team is moving toward long prompts, tool use, retrieval, agents, and frontier-model orchestration, while elite coding labs and open-model teams still use RLFT and specialized training heavily.

That distinction matters. Fine-tuning used to be the respectable answer when prompting was not enough. Now the burden of proof is higher: you fine-tune when you have durable data, a repeatable target behavior, and economics that beat context/tooling. Otherwise, you preserve flexibility in prompts, policies, tools, evals, and workflows.

My take: for consumer finance, the default should stay orchestration-first until the product has proprietary behavioral traces and stable task boundaries worth training against.

"The 'End' of a thing for most people does NOT mean the 'End' of a thing period."

Why it matters: Good discipline for deciding when personalization belongs in memory, retrieval, tool policy, or actual model training.
