Ethan Mollick: Use AI Without Surrendering the Human Work

One Useful Thing

Mollick's piece is the cleanest articulation in today's pile of the difference between AI as leverage and AI as abdication. He argues that AI writing, tutoring, and analysis go wrong when they remove the effort that creates taste, judgment, and learning; the useful version keeps the human in the loop and makes the work sharper.

The education examples are especially practical: plain ChatGPT helped students finish homework but hurt test performance, while an AI tutor that sequenced problems improved learning. The same pattern maps to product work: default assistants create output; good assistants create better humans and better decisions.

"Balancing using AI with our own mental abilities is going to be a defining challenge of the coming years."

Why it matters: Directly relevant to Hiro's assistant design. The product question is not just 'can the model answer?' but 'does this interaction preserve the user's agency, understanding, and confidence?'

Matt Levine: SpaceX May Force Index Funds to Choose Between Rules and the Market

Bloomberg Money Stuff

Levine frames the coming SpaceX IPO as a stress test for passive investing. If a huge public company has founder-favorable governance, thin float, eye-watering valuation, and massive market demand, index committees have to decide whether indexes represent all public-market value or only the securities that pass institutional quality filters.

His larger point is that passive investors do not really get to say no when the market anoints something large enough. That has obvious relevance beyond SpaceX: the same issue could show up with OpenAI, Anthropic, or any other giant AI company that reaches public markets with unusual governance or economics.

"The index provider, in this model, is sort of the collective voice of the market, able to push back on bad ideas from companies wanting to go public."

Why it matters: Useful lens for wealth products and portfolio advice: 'own the market' sounds simple until market structure, governance, and hype collide.
