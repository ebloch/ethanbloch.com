Fair Lending Is Not Going Away - It Is Fragmenting

Fintech Takes

Alex Johnson's fair-lending piece argues that the CFPB rollback of disparate impact under ECOA is less an ending than a shift in venue. Mortgages still sit under the Fair Housing Act, private plaintiffs still have ECOA claims, and state AGs in places like California, New York, Massachusetts, New Jersey, and Illinois are likely to keep effects-based enforcement alive. The likely result is not deregulation; it is a wider gap between large institutions that keep testing and smaller lenders or fintechs that pull back.

The sharper point for Hiro is that the old fair-lending toolkit was built for static underwriting models and inferred demographics. Cash-flow underwriting, high-dimensional transaction embeddings, and agentic optimization make the compliance problem more like AI safety than classic bank supervision. My take: this is exactly where a financial advisor or lender using AI needs defensible measurement, audit trails, and governance built into the product rather than bolted on by consultants later.

"The practical effect of the rule, in other words, may be that the floor drops while the ceiling stays roughly where it is, and the gap between the responsible and irresponsible ends of the industry widens."

Why it matters: Directly relevant to AI financial products: fair lending may become more state-driven, model-driven, and agent-risk-driven at the same time.

Agentic Engineering Is Starting to Feel Like a Black Box

Simon Willison

Simon Willison's most useful observation this week is not just that AI coding agents are getting better; it is that the boundary between responsible agentic engineering and irresponsible vibe coding is becoming harder to see from the outside, and sometimes from the inside. If an agent reliably writes routine code, engineers naturally stop reviewing every line and start treating the output like a trusted internal service.

That changes the evaluation problem. Tests, docs, and commit counts used to signal care; now they can be generated quickly. The new signal is whether software has actually been used, stressed, and operated. My take: for teams building with agents, provenance and operational history become more important than polish. The question moves from 'does the repo look serious?' to 'has this survived contact with real users and real failure modes?'

"Every time a model turns out to have written the right code without me monitoring it closely there's a risk that I'll trust it at the wrong moment in the future and get burned."

Why it matters: A concrete framing for how AI-native engineering teams should think about trust, review, and production accountability.
