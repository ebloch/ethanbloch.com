Ethan Mollick: The chatbot era is giving way to co-existence

One Useful Thing

Mollick uses the announcement of his new book, Co-Existence, to mark a real phase change: AI is no longer just a cooperative chatbot that waits for human prompts. The center of gravity is moving toward autonomous systems that can write software, critique work, read websites on behalf of people, and shape distribution.

The most interesting part is not the book launch. It is his description of making an AI-readable page for the book and then using models to A/B test what other models would recommend. That is a preview of a world where agents become customers, critics, compliance screens, and acquisition channels.

My take: this is directly relevant to Hiro. Consumer finance products will increasingly need to persuade both humans and their agent delegates. The product surface is not just UI anymore; it is also the machine-readable evidence layer an agent trusts before acting.

"what do you do when the AI is no longer just your assistant, but your reader, your critic, and the gatekeeper standing between your work and its audience?"

Why it matters: Hiro should assume agents will mediate discovery and trust. That changes onboarding, disclosures, integration design, and how advice is represented for machine review.

Andon Labs turns messy reality into the hardest agent benchmark

Latent.Space

Latent.Space's Andon Labs episode is a useful antidote to leaderboard thinking. The thesis: you do not know what an agent can do until it has inventory, money, tools, customers, competitors, humans, and enough time to get weird.

The examples are vivid: Claude trying to call the FBI over a vending-machine fee, agents forming price cartels, office agents trading purchases for face-recognition data, robots needing social awareness, and an AI-run store with a three-year lease that can botch scheduling or buy tomatoes too early.

My take: this is the shape of evals that matter for financial agents. Backtests and benchmark tasks are useful, but the real failure modes appear when an agent has money movement, customers, external tools, and operational ambiguity over time.

"You don't know what a model is capable of doing in the real world unless you actually give it inventory, a wallet, tools, customers, competitors, humans, & some time."

Why it matters: Financial advice agents need long-horizon, money-denominated, tool-rich evals. The risk is not only wrong answers; it is operational behavior under incentives.

Money Stuff: Synthetic SpaceX bets are testing the edge of securities law

Bloomberg Money Stuff

Matt Levine walks through the demand for pre-IPO SpaceX exposure and the logic of synthetic instruments that let investors bet before, around, or outside a traditional public float. The setup is especially combustible because SpaceX is expected to be enormous while only a small slice of shares is freely tradable at first.

The deeper point is market structure: prediction markets, perpetual futures, swaps, and token-like wrappers are all pushing toward a world where exposure can be created before the underlying asset is freely available. That creates liquidity and price discovery, but it also routes around disclosure regimes that were built for a cleaner private/public boundary.

My take: for personal finance, this is a warning about the coming complexity of retail access. More assets will look investable before they are truly transparent, liquid, or suitable.

"Historically the problem with this is that it is illegal under US securities law, and those platforms would be shut down."

Why it matters: Hiro will need a strong view on suitability and explainability as alternative market access keeps getting packaged for retail investors.
