Agentic banking is splitting into search boxes and real workflows

Fintech Takes

Alex Johnson frames Fifth Third's new app AI and Mercury Command as two versions of the same race: banks are realizing AI is not just a back-office productivity layer, but a new customer interface for financial tasks. Fifth Third is starting with navigation and intent routing inside a complex mobile app; Mercury is pushing further into agentic workflows for invoices, categorization, payments, cards, and business insights, with human approval before money moves.

The important difference is ambition. A bank can use AI to help users find the old interface, or it can let AI orchestrate the interface while deterministic rails handle regulated actions. That second model is much closer to where Hiro wants to live.

My take: the winning pattern in consumer and SMB finance will not be chat-only autonomy. It will be a controlled agent that can assemble task-specific UI, explain the action, and route the final step through permissioned payment/account rails.

"Command keeps you in control. The AI can never trigger actions on your account, it can only prompt users with actions that are then deterministically fired off by the user."

Why it matters: This is directly in Hiro's strike zone: AI as the financial UX layer, with explicit control and deterministic execution for sensitive actions.

AI security is becoming an enterprise deployment blocker, not a lab curiosity

Latent.Space

Latent.Space's interview with Zico Kolter and Matt Fredrikson is a useful state-of-the-market read on agent security: prompt injection, computer-use agents, guardrails, red-teaming arenas, identity, and insurance are converging into one enterprise problem. Their argument is that agents create a new exploit class because they combine untrusted data, private data, and tool access.

The interesting product angle is that security is moving from model behavior into platform design. Guardrails help, but so do isolation, scoped credentials, audit trails, and agent-native identities. The discussion around AI insurance is also worth watching: if insurers start requiring external assessments and mitigations, the compliance layer around AI agents could form very quickly.

My take: this is the same trust gap every serious financial agent has to cross. The question is not whether the model is smart enough; it is whether the surrounding system makes dangerous actions legible, bounded, and reversible.

"The first major public prompt-injection breach will probably do it."

Why it matters: Hiro will need security, identity, and permissioning as product primitives, not later enterprise checkboxes.

AI infrastructure financing is getting a public-company shortcut

Bloomberg Money Stuff

Matt Levine connects the AI data-center financing boom to SpaceX's first investment-grade bond sale. The broader point: lenders want steady cash flows, but AI buildouts are enormous and front-loaded. One workaround has been to have investment-grade public companies backstop data-center borrowing; another is to make the AI lab or infrastructure platform itself look like a large enough public credit.

SpaceX, in Levine's telling, is now both a giant AI startup and a giant public company, which makes the bond market more willing to look through current losses toward franchise strength, recurring Starlink cash flow, and AI upside. That is a template worth watching if more AI-native companies need debt markets to fund compute.

My take: the AI capex story is steadily becoming a capital markets story. The bottleneck is not just chips or power; it is whether investors can underwrite AI infrastructure as durable, financeable cash flow.

"This data center is leased to a trillion-dollar company: Yes, better."

Why it matters: Useful lens for AI infrastructure and macro: compute buildout will increasingly depend on credit narratives, not just venture or hyperscaler balance sheets.
