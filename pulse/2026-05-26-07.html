Nvidia Is Drawing a Line Between Hyperscalers and the Rest of the AI Stack

Stratechery

Ben Thompson's Nvidia earnings read is less about the headline growth number and more about where power accrues in the AI stack. Nvidia reported $81.6B in quarterly sales and framed demand around agentic AI, but the strategic move is its new reporting split: hyperscalers are large customers with bargaining power, while non-hyperscaler customers are more likely to buy Nvidia's integrated stack.

That distinction matters because the AI market is not one market. At the top, cloud giants will try to commoditize accelerators and own the customer. Everywhere else, Nvidia can sell a full system: chips, networking, software, reference architecture, and operational know-how. My take: this is the same question Hiro will face in miniature. The durable business is not just the model; it is the full workflow stack around a high-value user job.

"Demand has gone parabolic. The reason is simple: The era of agentic AI is here."

Why it matters: Good strategic lens for Hiro: value concentrates where the agent, tools, data, and workflow are packaged into one trusted system.

Everything, Everywhere Is Compliance

a16z

a16z makes the case that compliance is a giant, stubborn labor market waiting for agentic software. The useful numbers: more than 400,000 US compliance officers, over $40B in annual labor spend, and 33,300 projected annual openings. Banking is the most obvious wedge because every movement of money triggers a KYC, AML, reporting, disclosure, or customer communication obligation.

The argument is not that AI makes compliance easy. It is that the technology finally crossed from 'good enough to pilot' to 'good enough to trust' for document-heavy, rule-heavy work. Vision models, computer-use agents, and long-horizon task execution turn compliance from a dashboard problem into an operations problem. My take: financial advice will inherit this pattern. The winners will not just answer questions; they will carry regulated work across messy systems with auditability.

"Compliance is essentially applied legal reasoning under operational constraints."

Why it matters: Directly relevant to AI financial advice: trust, evidence, exceptions, and regulatory workflow are the product, not back-office details.

Fintech Bank Charters May Improve Economics and Compress Multiples

Fintech Takes

Alex Johnson ties together Mercury's conditional OCC approval, its $200M raise at a $5.2B valuation, Chime's open acknowledgement that a bank charter is likely inevitable, and SoFi's acquisition of Peach. The core point: fintechs may want charters for margin, control, and durability, but public investors often value banks on tangible book, not software-style revenue multiples.

The tension is clean. A charter can make the business better while making the story less tech-like. SoFi is the case study: once ZIRP ended and it became a bank, the multiple compressed before recovering to a still-bank-ish frame. My take: for consumer finance AI, the regulatory wrapper could be both moat and multiple tax. The product strategy has to earn enough trust and distribution to make that trade worth it.

"Tech investors don't ask what a business could be liquidated for. They ask what it could be worth in ten years."

Why it matters: Useful for thinking about Hiro's long-run boundary between software, fiduciary advice, banking infrastructure, and regulated financial institution economics.
