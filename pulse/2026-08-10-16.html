Situational Awareness Turns a Near Blow-Up Into a Silicon Valley Signal

Bloomberg / Money Stuff

Matt Levine's main story is the strange social physics around Leopold Aschenbrenner's Situational Awareness fund: after a near blow-up, some Silicon Valley investors reportedly wanted to put in more money. Wall Street reads the episode as leverage and risk management discipline arriving the hard way; Silicon Valley reads it as evidence that the manager is directionally right about AI and therefore worth backing through volatility.

The useful frame is not whether the trade was good. It is that AI capital formation is increasingly narrative-driven, and the same loss can be interpreted either as disqualifying risk or as proof that someone is close to the center of the next big thing.

My take: for Hiro, this is a sharp reminder that financial advice around AI-adjacent assets has to separate thesis confidence from portfolio construction. Being right about the future can still be a terrible client outcome if leverage, sizing, and timing are wrong.

"At the core, Silicon Valley rewards being directionally right about transformational technologies, while Wall Street rewards generating attractive adjusted returns while preserving capital."

Why it matters: This sits exactly at the intersection of AI narrative, wealth management, and investor behavior. It is a clean example of why advice needs to reason about both story and risk.

Scott Alexander's COVID Origins Bet Is Really About How Forecasts Change

Astral Codex Ten

In an ACX open thread, Scott Alexander says he won a $250K bet tied to whether new evidence would move superforecaster polling on COVID origins toward lab leak. The latest poll reportedly left forecasters favoring natural origins at roughly the same rate as two years earlier.

The interesting bit is Scott's stated meta-thesis: controversial questions rarely get decisive new evidence several years after the fact, and people rarely update without decisive evidence. That is more useful than the object-level COVID point.

My take: this is a compact lesson for prediction interfaces and advisor products. Updating users is not just about adding more information; it is about knowing when information is strong enough to overcome prior commitments.

"new and earth-shakingly decisive evidence on controversial events rarely comes out in the specific interval of 4-6 years after the event happened"

Why it matters: Relevant to markets, forecasting, and AI advisors: calibrated uncertainty is a product feature, not just a philosophical virtue.
