Anthropic's Safety Superpower Is Becoming a Power Struggle

Stratechery

Ben Thompson uses the Fable/Mythos shutdown to argue that Anthropic's safety posture is not just PR; it is the operating system that lets the company justify aggressive business, data, and policy moves. The immediate trigger is a U.S. export-control directive after reported jailbreak concerns, but the deeper story is Anthropic moving closer to the user while defending more control over how its frontier models are used.

The most useful frame for Hiro: frontier labs are fighting to own the workflow surface because usage data and feedback loops are becoming the scarce training asset. That is exactly the terrain where vertical software and agentic products have to decide what they own: customer relationship, permissions, private evals, trace data, and institutional memory.

My take: the safety story is powerful precisely because it is probably sincerely believed. That makes it more durable than ordinary positioning, and more dangerous for anyone building on top of a lab without a sovereignty plan.

"Anthropic's leadership effectively wants to have power over everything and everyone."

Why it matters: This is the strategic map for AI products: model access, data retention, workflow ownership, and regulatory legitimacy are now one coupled system. Hiro should treat model providers as critical infrastructure, not neutral vendors.

High-Volatility Stocks Are Having a Blow-Off Rally

Dan Rasmussen

Verdad flags an extreme factor reversal: since late March, low-volatility stocks are down roughly 13%, putting the trailing two-month underperformance in the 0.5th percentile since 1996. The rally has not just lifted semis and AI names; it has disproportionately rewarded lottery-like, high-volatility stocks.

Historically, this kind of move tends to show up in two regimes: rebounds off a bottom or late-cycle junk rallies. With AI enthusiasm still running hot and broader risk appetite snapping back after the Iran ceasefire, Verdad leans toward the uncomfortable interpretation: froth is rising under the surface.

My take: this is the market's version of a smoke alarm. It does not say the AI thesis is wrong, but it does say the marginal buyer is getting less discriminating.

"We’ll leave it up to our readers to decide whether today feels a lot more like a blow-off top than a bounce off the bottom."

Why it matters: Useful for Ethan's investing lens: the public market is separating less between durable AI earnings power and speculative risk appetite, which matters for product timing, personal allocation, and IPO context.

SpaceX's Real Plan Is Bigger Than Rockets

a16z

Alex Danco frames SpaceX from the endpoint backward: a self-sustaining Mars city, lunar industrial capacity, space-based compute, physical intelligence at scale, and cheap digital intelligence. The provocative bit is Musk's compensation package, which vests only if SpaceX reaches a $7.5 trillion valuation plus a million-person Mars colony, or if it operates space data centers drawing 100 terawatts of power.

The essay is partly sci-fi, but in a useful way. It connects SpaceX, Starlink, AI, robotics, energy, and orbital manufacturing into one industrial thesis rather than a set of separate bets. That makes the recent SpaceX IPO mania easier to understand: investors are not only buying rockets; they are buying an option on a different compute and energy frontier.

My take: the leap from orbital logistics to star-scale compute is enormous, but the mental model is valuable. The largest companies increasingly look like bundled infrastructure visions, not category leaders.

"The Banks Culture books are by far the best envisioning of an AI future."

Why it matters: The piece is a clean example of narrative power in markets: when a company owns the most ambitious possible story and has enough execution proof, valuation stops behaving like a spreadsheet exercise.
