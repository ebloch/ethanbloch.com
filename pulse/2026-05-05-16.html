Vibe Physics: AI Starts Extending the Research Frontier

Latent.Space

Latent.Space's Alex Lupsasca interview is the most consequential piece today: the AI story has moved from writing emails and code to accelerating theoretical physics. Lupsasca describes GPT-5 reproducing one of his best papers in 30 minutes, then helping solve a year-stalled amplitudes problem before a collaborator's plane landed.

The deeper point is not that AI is a better calculator. It is that expert taste plus model search can explore many more promising paths than one theorist could manually test. The human work shifts toward problem framing, verification, and knowing which generated line of thought is beautiful enough to keep.

My take: this is the same agent pattern showing up in a domain with a much higher bar than software. If the model can produce candidate knowledge faster than experts can validate it, the scarce resource becomes judgment and integration, not raw reasoning output.

"With AI able to output results basically as fast as we can conceive and validate them, the scope of what one theorist can hope to achieve has just gotten a lot, lot bigger."

Why it matters: Useful frame for Ethan's AI work: frontier value comes from pairing models with expert workflows where validation loops are strong and the upside from exploration is large.

Should Startups Go Long on Tokens or People?

a16z speedrun

a16z speedrun asks whether a seed-stage startup should spend serious money on AI agents instead of another engineer. The pro-token case is real: agents scale instantly, improve with the model curve, and can run workloads no human team would do at scale, like overnight security attacks against a codebase.

The pushback is sharper: AI gives every engineer more hands, but it does not replace ownership. Several founders converge on the same answer: the question is not tokens versus people, but which tasks benefit from automated throughput and which tasks need a person accountable for judgment, safety, architecture, and customer trust.

My take: the best line in the piece is that overspending tokens can signal imprecise thinking. Agent leverage starts before the prompt, in the decomposition of work.

"Spending more tokens usually just means the clarity of thought wasn't there when the task was started."

Why it matters: Directly relevant to building agentic product teams and deciding where Hiro/OpenAI-style agents should automate work versus preserve human accountability.

Financial Agents Are Colliding With Old Licensing Rules

Bloomberg Money Stuff

Matt Levine uses Morgan Stanley's Budapest analyst licensing issue to tee up the next version of the same question: if AI agents can draft pitch decks, review financial statements, and answer banking questions, where do they fit in a regulated profession?

Anthropic is releasing ten financial services agents aimed at banking, insurance, asset management, and fintech. Levine's joke lands because it is also a real product/regulatory question: if an AI agent walks a client through a model and deal process, is it just software, or is it performing licensed activity?

My take: this is exactly the kind of boundary financial AI products will keep hitting. The product surface can look like advice before the legal category has caught up.

"Will the AI agents have to get their Finra licenses? I'm pretty sure they could pass the exams."

Why it matters: Very relevant to AI financial advice: regulated workflows will need clean role definitions, auditability, and escalation paths before agent capability alone can be trusted.
