San Francisco Swaps a Housing Tax Cut for a Foreclosure Tax Fight

GrowSF

Mayor Daniel Lurie and Supervisor Bilal Mahmood have paused the BUILD Act, which would have cut San Francisco's unusually high transfer tax to make new apartment construction easier, and pivoted to a ballot measure taxing foreclosures on large commercial and multifamily properties. The proposal could raise a projected $200 million over three years, but a competing DSA-backed transfer-tax measure could cut that revenue in half and trigger litigation.

The deeper issue is that San Francisco is trying to solve a short-term budget hole without worsening the long-term recovery problem. A foreclosure tax may close a loophole on paper, but if it makes lenders extend bad loans instead of letting distressed buildings reset to new owners, it slows the downtown repair cycle.

My take: this is the exact kind of local policy tangle where the first-order fiscal story and second-order market behavior point in opposite directions. For Ethan, the interesting bit is not the tax itself; it's the incentive design under fiscal stress.

"A foreclosure isn't a market sale, and the tax comes out of the lender's recovery on a loan already gone bad."

Why it matters: Useful Bay Area operating context: housing production, distressed commercial real estate, and city budget math are converging into one ballot fight.

District 2 Rejects Anti-Growth Politics

GrowSF

Stephen Sherrill won the District 2 supervisor race decisively, taking 69.56% of the final ranked-choice vote against Lori Brooke. GrowSF frames the result as a clean rejection of anti-upzoning politics: Brooke leaned into opposition to upzoning, while Sherrill backed Mayor Lurie's Family Zoning Plan.

The numbers behind the race are more interesting than the personalities. GrowSF cites polling showing 84% District 2 support for Family Zoning and broad citywide support for mid-rise housing in the Sunset and Richmond plus towers downtown, SoMa, and Mission Bay.

My take: this is another data point that SF's political median has shifted toward pragmatic abundance, at least when the question is posed concretely. That matters for anyone trying to build, hire, or simply live in the Bay Area.

"They want more homes, and they want leaders willing to say yes out loud."

Why it matters: A real local-politics signal: pro-housing politics are not just activist discourse; they are winning elections in wealthy SF neighborhoods.
