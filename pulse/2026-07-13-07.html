Apple's OpenAI Lawsuit Looks Like a Symptom, Not the Disease

Stratechery

Ben Thompson's read is that one former Apple employee may have behaved badly, but the larger story is Apple's strategic discomfort. OpenAI's device work threatens Apple's role as the consumer AI gateway, while Apple's own AI product execution keeps slipping.

The useful lens for Ethan: this is less about trade secrets and more about distribution. AI companies want direct consumer relationships; Apple wants to preserve its hardware-mediated interface layer. That tension is going to shape assistant UX, device partnerships, and recruiting norms across the AI stack.

My take: the lawsuit is a noisy artifact of a much quieter problem for Apple. The more agents become the interface, the less defensible the old app-and-device control points become.

"This case is about Apple's former employees stealing Apple's trade secrets for the benefit of OpenAI."

Why it matters: For Hiro, the strategic question is distribution and trust: whoever owns the daily assistant surface can route financial workflows, not just answer questions.

The Case for Indexing US Large Caps Is Getting Harder to Argue Against

Dan Rasmussen

Dan Rasmussen argues US large-cap equities are the world's most efficient market: enormous, liquid, heavily researched, and nearly impossible for active managers to beat after fees. Morningstar's 10-year data through 2025 showed only 3.6% of US large-growth active funds and 8.1% of large-blend active funds survived and beat their index.

His practical conclusion is not anti-active management. It is budget discipline: spend active risk where neglect is higher, like small caps, Japan small/mid caps, or high yield, and default to cheap beta where the base rates are brutal.

My take: this is the kind of portfolio principle that should be embedded in consumer advice. The question is not whether active management can work in theory; it is whether the user is being paid enough odds to take the complexity.

"My mental model is simple: alpha is a function of neglect."

Why it matters: Directly relevant to Hiro's advice engine: asset allocation should start from base rates, not product availability or manager storytelling.
