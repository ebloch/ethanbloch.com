AI Engineering Is Now About Harnesses, Loops, and Skills

Latent.Space

Latent.Space's AI Engineer World's Fair recap says the field has moved past the 2023 agent demo phase. The interesting work is now in the systems around models: harnesses, context management, evals, permissions, long-running jobs, and human decision loops. That is exactly the operational layer where agent products either become reliable software or stay impressive demos.

The conference language is converging around 'loop engineering' and 'skills.' Agents run the inner execution loop; engineers design the outer loop, decide when humans review, and encode reusable judgment into workflow files. My take: this is the important maturation step for Hiro-style products too. The moat is not just model access; it is whether the product can encode financial judgment, controls, integrations, and review into a system that improves without getting loose.

"The agent runs the inner execution loop; I set the direction and I make decisions in the outer loop."

Why it matters: This maps directly to building trusted financial agents: the product has to be a harness for judgment, not a chat box that occasionally uses tools.

AI Chip Regulation Is Being Reframed as Industrial Compliance, Not a Panopticon

Astral Codex Ten

Scott Alexander argues that Plan A-style AI chip controls should be evaluated like other high-friction industrial regulation: registration, inspections, secure facilities, resale controls, and transparency for large training runs. His core point is not that the rules are free, but that regulating a tiny number of chip fabs, hyperscalers, and frontier data centers is much less like consumer surveillance than critics claim.

The harder tradeoff is open weights. He treats a future ban on training new open-weight frontier models as a real freedom cost, while noting that economic and security pressure may be pushing that way regardless. My take: the useful frame is power diffusion versus open release. If frontier open weights become untenable, the replacement mechanism has to be credible diffusion of algorithms, compute access, and institutional checks, or the cure concentrates power anyway.

"These are substantial. But none of them involve a surveillance state or a dystopia, just standard government regulation levers like auditing factories and imposing taxes."

Why it matters: Good policy calibration for anyone building AI products in regulated domains: compliance can be annoying without being dystopian, but open ecosystem costs are real.

The Best AI Productivity Advice Is Still: Offload Chores, Keep Judgment

Platformer

Casey Newton interviews The Verge's David Pierce, who has tried roughly 200 to-do apps and is deeply skeptical of AI tools that promise to run your life. Pierce's view is refreshingly practical: AI is useful for annoying maintenance work, file conversions, inbox cleanup, first-pass research, and finding service providers. It is much worse at deciding what matters to you.

That distinction matters because AI product adoption is being pulled between real utility and productivity theater. The better products reduce friction around capture, search, and transformation while preserving a user's single source of truth. My take: financial AI should steal this lesson. Automate the annoying parts, but make the prioritization and tradeoffs legible enough that the user still owns the judgment.

"This idea that everyone is selling — that you have to either get on the AI train or get left behind — is pernicious and frankly wrong."

Why it matters: Useful counterweight to agent maximalism. Hiro wins by making financial work easier and clearer, not by pretending to replace personal judgment.
