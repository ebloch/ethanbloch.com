AI Capex Is Splitting Winners From Storytellers

Stratechery

Ben Thompson reads Google and Meta's earnings as a live test of AI infrastructure spending. Google is getting rewarded because Cloud revenue hit $20 billion, grew 63% year over year, and showed margin expansion; Meta is getting punished despite strong core results because Zuckerberg is asking investors to underwrite a long personal-agent vision before the payoff is visible.

The Anthropic angle is the most interesting part. Google may be benefiting twice: from a mark-up on its Anthropic stake and from Anthropic-driven demand for TPUs and Google Cloud. My take: this is the enterprise-AI version of distribution leverage. The company that owns both the compute platform and a preferred route to frontier-model usage can show revenue before the market fully understands where the usage is coming from.

"Our Enterprise AI solutions have become our primary growth driver for Cloud for the first time."

Why it matters: Useful for Ethan's OpenAI finance work because it frames the investor question clearly: AI spend is tolerated when it turns into measurable revenue, backlog, or distribution advantage.

Economic Security Needs a Dual Mandate, Not a Vulnerability List

ChinaTalk

ChinaTalk's essay proposes two headline metrics for economic security: Chokepoint Exposure Index, the share of GDP at risk from adversary-controlled supply-chain nodes, and Mobilization Elasticity, the ability to surge critical production within 180 days without intolerable price spikes. The argument is that America keeps spending on flagship industrial policy while lacking a dashboard for whether the money actually reduces physical fragility.

The strongest example is munitions: Congress can appropriate billions, but if the U.S. cannot make TNT, propellants, casings, or transformers quickly, money does not convert into output. My take: this is a crisp product idea for policy. It turns economic security from vibes and lists into a scoreboard with triggers, owners, and levers.

"Money is not a magic wand for manufacturing; in a crisis, you cannot simply legislate or spend your way out of the physical reality of industrial systems."

Why it matters: This maps directly to finance-product thinking: make the hidden constraint measurable, then tie actions to thresholds.

Japan Turned Its Public Balance Sheet Into a Levered Equity Fund

Dan Rasmussen

Verdad argues Japan's scary gross debt picture misses the asset side. Since 2012, the Bank of Japan and GPIF accumulated large equity and foreign-security positions, creating a de facto sovereign wealth fund funded by low-cost liabilities. The result: public net liabilities fell from 117% of GDP in 2012 to 65% in 2025, even while primary deficits continued.

That makes Japan's corporate-governance push more than micro reform. If the state owns a large slice of the market, higher returns on capital and better capital allocation strengthen the sovereign balance sheet itself. My take: it is a fascinating inversion of fiscal policy, where equity-market reform becomes a macro stabilization tool.

"Japan funds itself cheaply at short-duration, floating-rate liabilities and uses the public balance sheet to hold long-duration, risky assets."

Why it matters: Good investing and macro fodder: it connects governance reform, national balance sheets, and the fiscal value of market structure.
