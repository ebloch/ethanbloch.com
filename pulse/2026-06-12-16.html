Prediction markets are forcing regulators to admit what feels like finance

Fintech Takes

Alex Johnson's read of the CFTC's 267-page prediction-market proposal is that it is less a clean regulatory theory than a map of what the agency wants to tolerate: sports outcome contracts survive, death and terror markets get carved out, and weird casino-like products such as mentions markets mostly slip through the cracks. The key tension is that the rule uses economic-utility language when it wants to bless sports markets, then switches to moral intuition when it wants to ban adjacent ugly cases.

The useful framework he surfaces is 'determinative capacity': whether one person can control the contract outcome. That is a better organizing principle than the current vibes-based distinction between acceptable gambling, useful hedging, and socially repugnant contracts.

My take: this matters for Hiro because consumer finance keeps acquiring gambling mechanics under respectable wrappers. Prediction markets are the cleanest current example of finance, entertainment, and regulation converging faster than the rulebook can honestly describe.

"Put simply, it’s regulation by vibes."

Why it matters: A sharp fintech-regulatory case study: when product-market fit comes from speculation, regulators often retrofit principles around the market instead of starting from consumer outcomes.

SpaceX's IPO debate is really about the stay-private-forever cycle

Bloomberg Money Stuff

The latest Money Stuff podcast circles SpaceX IPO pricing, meme demand, index divergence, S&P's no-fast-tracking decision, and the incentives that let extraordinary companies avoid public markets for longer. The surface topic is SpaceX, but the broader issue is how late-stage private markets now absorb much of the growth, governance, and fandom that public markets used to intermediate.

For investors, that makes benchmark construction and access feel increasingly weird: the most culturally important companies can stay outside the indices until after much of the compounding has happened, while public investors get a narrower and sometimes more distorted opportunity set.

My take: the relevance is less 'will SpaceX IPO?' and more 'what should a personal finance product tell users when the public market is no longer the full market?'

"the pricing of the SpaceX initial public offering, cash-settled derivatives gambling, S&P’s no-fast-tracking decision, index divergence"

Why it matters: Useful backdrop for Hiro's investing narrative: access, diversification, and benchmark advice are harder when iconic growth companies remain private longer.
