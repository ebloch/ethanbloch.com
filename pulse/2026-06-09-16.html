Ethan Mollick: Mythos Models Turn Users Into Patrons

One Useful Thing

Mollick's early access writeup on Claude 5 Fable is the clearest description of the next agentic threshold: models that can work for many hours, spawn helper agents, research, code, test, and return complete artifacts from ambitious briefs. The striking part is not just capability, but the changed human role. Mollick says he increasingly felt less like an operator and more like a client commissioning a studio.

For Hiro, the useful distinction is control versus leverage. If frontier agents become powerful but opaque, the product problem shifts toward scoping, verification, auditability, and taste. In regulated or high-trust domains, the assistant cannot simply disappear into a black box and return a polished answer. The winning interface may be the one that gives users confidence without forcing them to watch every intermediate step.

"I no longer steer; I commission."

Why it matters: This is directly relevant to Hiro's agent design: customers will want the leverage of autonomous work, but financial advice still needs inspectable reasoning, constraints, and evidence.

New York Tech Week Shows AI Adoption Moving East

a16z speedrun

A16z's field notes from New York Tech Week are a useful adoption snapshot: 50,000 attendees, 1,500 events, and a sense that New York is now becoming genuinely AI-pilled. The most interesting detail is the regional lag. A16z investor Troy Kirwin says NYC knowledge workers are now adopting Claude Code and Cowork while SF peers have already moved on to Codex-style workflows.

That lag matters because it means the agent boom is no longer only an SF-native builder phenomenon. The next wave is non-technical knowledge workers and enterprise buyers discovering what these tools can do, but doing it with local cultural norms around trust, referrals, events, and professional identity.

"NY is becoming AI pilled, but there is definitely a 4-6 month lag to counterparts in SF."

Why it matters: This is a go-to-market signal for Hiro: AI-native finance tools may need different adoption motions in SF, New York, and mainstream professional markets.

Matt Levine: Investors Can Just Not Buy SpaceX

Bloomberg Money Stuff

Levine uses SpaceX's expected public-market governance structure to poke at a strange assumption in modern indexing: large institutions feel obligated to own everything and then govern it from within. His counter is brutally simple. If the valuation or governance is unacceptable, do not buy the stock. The fact that this sounds complicated for index-linked public pensions says more about modern market structure than about SpaceX.

The broader point is useful beyond Musk. Universal ownership has created institutions that think they must accept exposure first and negotiate rights second. SpaceX is testing whether investors still have real exit discipline when a culturally important, index-adjacent asset tells them they will have no meaningful voice.

"Your main defense against SpaceX's governance and valuation is not to buy the stock."

Why it matters: Useful investment framing: governance risk is not abstract if the investor's own mandate makes exit hard. That matters for any public-market product trying to explain risk honestly.
