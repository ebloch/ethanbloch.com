Open USD Turns Stablecoins Into a Payments Consortium War

Fintech Takes

Visa, Stripe, Mastercard, BlackRock, Coinbase and 140+ companies are backing Open USD, a neutral stablecoin that shares reserve income with participating businesses. Alex Johnson's read is the important one: this is not about building a fat-margin stablecoin issuer, it is about payments incumbents preventing Circle and Tether from becoming the interface layer for global programmable money.

The governance questions are the risk. Libra died because a consortium looked too much like private monetary policy; Open USD arrives in a market where crypto has more political cover, Stripe has better credibility than Meta, and everyone has learned to describe the thing as utility infrastructure. My take: for Hiro, the useful frame is that stablecoins are becoming a platform primitive, not a product category. The winners will own workflow, trust, risk controls, and distribution on top.

"The point isn't the stablecoin. The point is that a neutral, jointly-owned stablecoin lets each of them keep winning in the areas where they already win."

Why it matters: Directly relevant to AI finance. If money movement becomes an open utility rail, the durable margin shifts to advice, orchestration, fraud/risk, and embedded user trust.

Prediction Markets Are Creating Incentives to Hack Reality

Bloomberg Money Stuff

Matt Levine walks through the Kalshi/Spotify mess: Spotify removed 500,000 artificial streams from Malcolm Todd's 'Earrings,' but the inflated numbers had already settled a Kalshi market with $3 million of trading. If fake streams cost hundreds of dollars and prediction-market payoffs can reach tens of thousands, the rational attack is not to predict reality but to cheaply alter the measured proxy.

This is the darker version of 'truth machines.' Prediction markets reward information discovery, but they also create new profit pools around manipulating tiny pieces of reality: streams, weather gauges, words at press conferences, even the location of a gorilla. My take: this rhymes with AI evaluation and financial benchmarks. Once a metric pays, adversaries optimize the measurement surface before they optimize the underlying truth.

"Prediction markets can make it harder to know what is true."

Why it matters: Useful product-risk lesson for Hiro: any autonomous financial system that routes money based on external signals needs adversarial thinking about data provenance, incentives, and metric manipulation.
