AI Companies Are Relearning Pyramids

Bloomberg Money Stuff

Matt Levine's core point: frontier AI companies need enormous outside capital while their founders are unusually allergic to giving up control. SpaceX's IPO pushed the modern answer to an extreme with super-voting stock, board appointment rights, and limited shareholder litigation rights.

The older answer is pyramiding: stack holding companies so a founder can raise capital at multiple layers while preserving voting control through a chain. DeepSeek's reported $7.4B round echoes that logic by putting investor capital into a limited partnership managed by Liang Wenfeng rather than directly into DeepSeek.

My take: this is the governance side of the AI capex boom. The market is currently willing to fund structures it would hate in calmer sectors because compute, talent, and perceived strategic stakes are overriding normal investor protections.

"The goals of an AI founder are (1) to raise as much money from investors as possible and (2) to give those investors as little control as possible."

Why it matters: Useful frame for OpenAI/Anthropic/AI infra financing: control rights are becoming part of product-market fit for capital, not just legal plumbing.

How to Pivot When AI Keeps Moving the Ground

a16z speedrun

a16z speedrun argues that AI has made pivoting a recurring founder skill because model capabilities shift every quarter. The best pivots they highlight keep one foot planted: Clay kept the spreadsheet/data-chaining insight and moved toward GTM; Lovable used open-source demand as signal but rebuilt for nontechnical users; Cursor left CAD for a market the founders actually understood.

The practical test is blunt: try to book fifty customer conversations in two weeks. If nobody will talk, or fewer than 15-20% convert into serious discovery, that is signal rather than a messaging problem.

My take: the Hiro-relevant version is to distinguish 'the model got better' from 'the customer behavior changed.' The first creates demos; the second creates businesses.

"The most successful founders are truth-seekers."

Why it matters: Directly relevant to AI product strategy: rapid prototyping only matters if it tightens the loop with actual user pull.

The Learning-Helmet Result Did Not Replicate

Astral Codex Ten

An ACX grantee spent about $32,000 trying to replicate a 2023 neuroscience result claiming individualized alpha-frequency light flicker could boost perceptual learning. The replication found similar learning rates across groups and argues the original effect was likely an artifact of noisy summary statistics and outlier handling.

The larger lesson is methodological: the original paper's aggregate chart made the effect look much cleaner than the underlying participant-level data apparently warranted. A careful look at raw-ish distributions could have flagged the weakness before a full replication.

My take: this is a good anti-hype story for biohacking and AI-adjacent productivity claims. If an intervention sounds like a personalized optimization miracle, ask whether the variance is doing all the work.

"And no, it didn't replicate. Most likely it can't replicate, because the effect is probably not real."

Why it matters: A useful epistemic check for product claims around personalization, measurement, and human performance.
