Chinese Open Models Make AI a Cost-Structure Game

Stratechery

Ben Thompson's Kimi K3 piece is the clearest read today because it separates model R&D from serving economics. Open weights can make model creation feel cheap, but the business still lives or dies on COGS: memory footprint, serving efficiency, token efficiency, and how many reasoning tokens it takes to produce the same useful answer.

That matters because Kimi K3 and Alibaba's Qwen3.8 are pushing the market toward a world where 'intelligence' for many tasks becomes more commodity-like. If models converge on capability, the winner is not automatically the lab with the best demo; it is the provider with the best marginal cost and distribution. My take: this is exactly the lens to use for Hiro too. The product value is not just model quality, it is the workflow, trust layer, and cost-efficient orchestration around the model.

"What is fungible is what is constructed from tokens, which is to say intelligence."

Why it matters: Reframes the China model panic into unit economics. For agent products, model choice becomes an operating margin and latency decision, not a religious commitment.

Agent Payments Are Getting a Real Standards Push

TLDR Fintech

More than 40 finance and technology companies have joined the Linux Foundation's x402 Foundation, an open governance effort for web-native payments. The standard was originally contributed by Coinbase and is meant to let AI agents, APIs, and applications transact as easily as they exchange data, across cards, stablecoins, and other payment methods.

The same TLDR edition also flags Stripe and Advent's reported $53B bid for PayPal, Visa's stablecoin platform, and Visa's banking-app AI assistant. The connective tissue is obvious: payments networks are trying to own the rails before agents start making authenticated, user-approved economic decisions.

"If widely adopted, x402 could become foundational infrastructure for AI-driven commerce."

Why it matters: Directly relevant to Hiro: advice is only half the job if agents eventually compare, switch, pay, rebalance, cancel, and renew on the user's behalf. The approval and payment layers become product surface area.

Public Microcaps Look Like the Neglected Version of Private Equity

Verdad Weekly Research

Dan Rasmussen argues that investors agree small companies are fertile ground for active management, then express that belief almost entirely through private equity. Meanwhile, public microcaps trade at steep discounts: about 20% below the broad U.S. equity market in the U.S., 30% in Europe, 40% in Korea, and 50% in Japan, according to Verdad's work.

The useful provocation is that the discount is not obviously explained by deteriorating fundamentals. Verdad says microcap fundamentals have held up versus broader benchmarks, implying neglect rather than impairment. My take: this is a good example of institutional preference becoming a valuation anomaly. Allocators have confused access and illiquidity with opportunity.

"The smallest public companies remain unusually cheap not because investors have rejected the idea that smaller companies are attractive but because they have chosen to pursue those companies exclusively through private equity."

Why it matters: Good investing mental model: when everyone wants the same exposure through the same wrapper, check the unloved wrapper next door.
