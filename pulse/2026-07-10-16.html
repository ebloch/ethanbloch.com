GPT-5.6 Turns the Model Race Toward Long-Running Agents

Simon Willison's Newsletter

Simon Willison’s read on GPT-5.6 is useful because it separates launch theater from product surface area. Luna, Terra, and Sol arrive with million-token context, 128k output limits, and pricing that makes the smaller models central to the story, not just cheaper fallbacks. The bigger claim is agentic: OpenAI is pointing at long-running professional workflows as the benchmark frontier, with Sol leading Agents' Last Exam and the smaller models pitched as affordable intelligence at scale.

The most interesting part for builders is not the model ladder; it is the API substrate around it. Programmatic tool calling lets models compose JavaScript to orchestrate tools, multi-agent support formalizes parallel subagents, and explicit prompt cache breakpoints give developers more control over cost and latency. My take: the frontier is moving from chat quality to operating-system behavior. Hiro should care less about which model wins a headline benchmark and more about which stack can reliably plan, call tools, preserve state, and recover from partial failure.

"Programmatic Tool Calling allows the models to 'compose and run JavaScript that orchestrates tool calls'"

Why it matters: Directly relevant to Hiro’s agent architecture: durable financial advice depends on tool use, multi-step work, and cost control more than raw single-turn cleverness.

The Bank Charter Window Is Open, But the Culture Bar Is High

Fintech Takes

Fintech Takes runs an anonymous essay from a community bank CEO on the sudden surge of interest in bank charters from fintechs, neobanks, crypto firms, stablecoin players, and even car companies. The author’s frame is blunt: if you want to own or run a bank, you need a Lisa Simpson culture, not a Bart culture. Compliance is not a costume you put on for regulators; it has to be the operating system.

That sounds obvious until you map it onto fintech incentives. A lot of startup value has been created by rule-bending, regulatory ambiguity, and moving faster than the supervisory perimeter. The essay’s sharper point is that a bank charter reverses that bargain: trust, controls, customer protection, and self-reporting mistakes become the product. My take: this is a useful reminder that financial AI products will inherit the same trust burden, even when they do not hold a charter themselves.

"You need to be Lisa Simpson. That’s it. It’s not complicated."

Why it matters: Hiro’s product surface sits in high-trust financial territory. The durable advantage is not just intelligence; it is being visibly, structurally trustworthy.

China’s AI Infrastructure Is Moving to the Exurbs, Not the Mythic West

ChinaTalk

ChinaTalk argues that China’s 'Eastern Data, Western Compute' slogan misdescribes the actual geography of AI infrastructure. The practical pattern is not a clean westward relocation of compute; it is exurban clustering around already-rich eastern metros, where chips, skilled labor, network connectivity, and operations ecosystems exist. Cheap land and power matter, but they are not the dominant cost driver when chips and construction do most of the work.

The darker implication is regional development theater. Poorer interior provinces were sold a story that data centers could become a new growth engine, and some appear to have built capacity ahead of real demand. My take: this is the AI boom’s version of industrial policy meeting utilization math. Compute is not a commodity you sprinkle evenly across a map; it is a dense ecosystem with supply chains, talent, latency, and customers attached.

"Perhaps the slogan should not be 'Eastern Data, Western Compute,' but 'Eastern Data, 45 Minutes Outside the City Compute.'"

Why it matters: Useful macro context for AI infrastructure investing: power is important, but utilization, chips, operations talent, and customer proximity decide whether capacity is real or stranded.
