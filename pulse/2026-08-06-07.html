Inference engineering is becoming its own product discipline

Latent.Space

Latent.Space's Baseten deep dive is the most practical AI infrastructure read in today's set: the frontier has moved from 'can this model answer?' to 'can this model answer fast, reliably, cheaply, and under ugly production constraints?' The interesting bit is how much performance is still hiding in serving: cache-aware routing, disaggregated prefill/decode, speculative decoding, quantization, kernels, and dedicated deployments can still produce 20%, 100%, or even 200% gains.

For Hiro, the takeaway is that inference is now part of product strategy, not a backend afterthought. If financial advice becomes agentic, latency and cost curves determine what workflows can feel conversational versus what has to be batched, queued, or simplified. My take: the companies that treat model serving as a first-class design surface will build noticeably different products than teams just swapping API calls.

"Inference is no longer just the final step after training. It is becoming its own engineering discipline, with its own research problems, infrastructure, and increasingly specialized roles."

Why it matters: Hiro's experience quality will depend on agent latency, reliability, and cost. This is a useful map of where those constraints actually come from.

Agents need verifiers, not just loops

a16z

This essay gets at the core engineering problem behind useful agents: an agent can always take another step, so the hard part is deciding whether the next step is likely to improve the result. The author frames completion as something supplied by the surrounding system: tests, specs, approvals, risk thresholds, deadlines, and observable state.

The useful distinction is between loops that merely retry and loops that converge. A test suite can make a coding agent look successful while it overfits visible tests; visual generation can iterate forever without a local edit path; open-ended work needs a representation of 'done.' My take: this is exactly where consumer finance agents will either earn trust or feel sloppy. The verifier cannot be vibes; it needs grounded account data, policy constraints, user preferences, and explicit confidence boundaries.

"The verifier is not just the stop condition. It also defines what the loop treats as progress."

Why it matters: Personal finance advice is full of ambiguous stopping points. Hiro needs clear verification loops for recommendations, follow-ups, and handoffs.

ChatGPT Work sketches the agent platform shape

Latent.Space

Shlok Khemani's outside-in teardown of ChatGPT Work is a useful product anatomy lesson: cloud computers, persistent task workspaces, file artifacts, browser services, personal context, app/plugin routing, and scheduled tasks are converging into one agent operating model. The reported early adoption number is striking, but the more important point is architectural: agents are becoming long-running workers with memory, tools, files, and permission ledgers.

For Ethan, the interesting angle is not just OpenAI platform strategy; it is how quickly expectations are moving. Users will increasingly expect an advisor to remember context, manipulate documents, pull data from accounts, and complete workflows across services without treating every task as a fresh chat.

"Three weeks in, Work (along with Codex) has reportedly crossed 10 million users."

Why it matters: This is directly adjacent to Hiro's product surface: trusted agents, persistent context, integrations, and safe execution.
