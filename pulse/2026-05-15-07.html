The Burden Is Shifting Back to AI Slowdown Arguments

Astral Codex Ten

Scott Alexander's core point is not that AI progress must keep compounding forever. It is that saying every exponential becomes a sigmoid is not a forecast; it is a placeholder for an actual model. The essay walks through repeated cases where forecasters saw a curve and prematurely called the flattening point: UN birthrate projections, solar deployment, and a Wharton analysis of METR-style AI task horizons.

The useful frame for Ethan is evidentiary. If someone argues AI capability growth will flatten before scary or economically transformative thresholds, they need either a mechanistic account of the bottleneck or a strong prior for why this trend is about to break now. In the absence of that, Alexander says the black-box prior should look more Lindy: a trend that has persisted for years deserves a baseline expectation of persisting longer.

My take: this is a good antidote to both lazy doom and lazy dismissal. For product planning, it argues for robust strategy under continued capability gains rather than betting the roadmap on a conveniently timed plateau.

"all exponentials eventually become sigmoids"

Why it matters: Hiro is exposed to model capability curves, agent reliability, and user trust. The practical question is not whether progress eventually slows, but what must be true before it is safe to plan around that slowdown.

Memory Is the Newest AI Infrastructure Bottleneck

a16z

a16z's charts show AI demand spreading from GPUs and power into DRAM, HBM, and NAND. Monthly DRAM contract prices have more than tripled year over year, NAND has roughly doubled, and memory-makers are expected to sextuple operating income in 2026. A previously cyclical, commodity-like layer of the stack is suddenly showing pricing power.

The strategic tension is whether this is a one-time shortage or a structural step-change. If the AI compute buildout keeps compounding, memory stops looking like a commodity cycle and starts looking like another scarce input with geopolitical, capex, and product-cost consequences. If costs spike enough, the incentive to optimize around memory gets stronger too: quantization, TPUs, Trainium, and software efficiency become part of the same supply-chain story.

My take: this is a reminder that the AI cost curve is not only model weights and GPU rental. The real constraint moves around the system, and product teams that understand the full stack will have better instincts about margins and latency.

"memory has entered the bottleneck chat"

Why it matters: Personal-finance agents need low-cost, responsive inference. Memory pressure affects both the economics of serving useful agents and the shape of infrastructure moats.

Coding Agents Are Converging on the Same Mission-Control Interface

AINews / Latent Space

AINews argues that the newest coding-agent products are independently arriving at the Conductor-style form factor: a mission-control surface for parallel agent work, repo/PR lifecycle management, steering, and review. GitHub's new app, Codex mobile, remote SSH, hooks, programmatic access tokens, and VS Code multi-agent UX all point toward the same product grammar.

That convergence matters because the market is shifting from single chat windows to managed workstreams. The differentiator may be less 'can it write code?' and more whether teams can safely spawn, observe, interrupt, evaluate, and integrate many agents doing real work across real repositories.

My take: this is agent product-market fit becoming UI convention. Once every serious agent product grows the same cockpit, the moat moves to trust, permissions, eval loops, local context, and workflow ownership.

"Everything is Conductor"

Why it matters: Ethan works inside the same agent adoption wave. The lesson generalizes beyond code: financial agents will also need a control surface for delegation, review, approvals, and traceability.
