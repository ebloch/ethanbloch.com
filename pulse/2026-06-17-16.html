Fraud AI Should Strengthen Rules, Not Replace Judgment

Fintech Business Weekly

Kunal Datta's guest piece argues that fraud teams keep chasing the visible attack surface -- deepfakes, phishing variants, behavioral biometrics -- while the stable point is still money movement. His case is that traditional ML is excellent at known patterns but structurally slow against novel fraud: it needs large labeled datasets, produces opaque scores, and waits for retraining cycles.

The more interesting proposal is using LLMs to turn analyst notes and dispositions into evolving, auditable detection logic. That frames AI as a feedback layer around rules: one or two examples can become proposed logic, false positives can become filters, and the fraud team stays in control. My take: this is the right shape for high-stakes fintech AI -- augment the workflow where human reasoning already exists, then make the output inspectable enough for compliance and operations.

"This is not AI replacing the analyst. It is AI thinking with the analyst."

Why it matters: Directly relevant to Hiro: financial AI will win less by making black-box scores and more by turning messy human financial reasoning into reliable, reviewable workflows.

The Self-Driving Lab Comes for Materials Science

Latent.Space

Latent.Space's Radical AI interview is a useful counterweight to naive 'AI will design everything' narratives. Materials are not just token strings; performance depends on supply chains, microstructures, manufacturing process, and reproducibility. Radical's bet is a closed-loop lab system that proposes experiments, runs them, learns from results, and tries to accelerate discovery by more than 10x versus traditional programs.

The broader insight is that AI in the physical world looks less like a magic model and more like an integrated operating system: experiments, instruments, data capture, and domain experts all in the loop. My take: the same lesson applies to financial advice. The moat is not only the model; it is the feedback-rich system around the model.

"We count it as a discovery when you pick up your phone and there’s a new material sitting inside of it."

Why it matters: A clean example of agentic systems tied to real-world outcomes, not demos. Useful analogy for building AI products where the real value is validated behavior over time.

AI Lottery Tickets Move from VC to Public Markets

Money Stuff

Matt Levine uses Allbirds' transformation into Smartbird to explain a public-market version of AI startup speculation. A struggling public company can announce an AI infrastructure pivot, sell stock into the excitement through an ATM or floating-strike convertible, and raise capital from retail investors who cannot access the buzzy private AI rounds.

The comedy is the point: private VCs are already funding vague AI companies based on team pedigree and narrative, so public markets are creating their own rough equivalent. My take: this is a good bubble sensor. When capital formation starts rewarding the phrase 'AI infrastructure' more than demonstrated product-market fit, the marginal buyer is underwriting story velocity.

"The public stock market, for reasons of its own, has created a version of AI startup investing."

Why it matters: Good market-structure context for the AI cycle: capital is chasing access to the theme through whatever wrapper is available.
