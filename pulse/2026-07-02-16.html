Adobe's Agentic Website Assembles Itself for Each Visitor

AINews

Adobe Principal Scientist Carlos Sanchez showed an "agentic site" that interprets visitor intent, retrieves from a company's existing content corpus, and composes a page in real time. The important detail is not just personalization; it is that the website becomes a governed content-and-interaction system rather than a fixed tree of pages.

The economics are starting to look plausible: Sanchez estimated inference cost at one to two cents per generated page, with a latency target of one to two seconds. For Hiro, this points toward an interface pattern where a financial product does not just personalize recommendations, but assembles the right explanation, workflow, and calls to action based on what the user is trying to do.

My take: the strongest version of this is not a marketing trick. It is a bridge between human UI and agent-readable tools, where the same underlying product surface can serve a person browsing visually, a delegated agent negotiating with structured endpoints, or a chat experience rendering task-specific UI.

"Many people don't even think it's possible to generate a web page on the fly. People think it is future-looking. No, you can do this. It's not the future, it's the present now."

Why it matters: Directly relevant to Hiro's product surface: personal finance has high intent variance, rich source material, and lots of moments where the right UI should be assembled around the task rather than predesigned as one generic funnel.

Skill Engineering Is Emerging as the Control Layer for Agents

AINews

Paul Bakaus, creator of Impeccable, argues that AI agents need domain vocabulary, context, and steering mechanisms rather than bigger one-shot prompts. His design skill turns vague adjectives like "bolder" or "quieter" into operational design moves, so agents can improve interfaces inside an existing system without drifting into generic AI taste.

The deeper point is that skills are becoming a product primitive: a way to encode professional judgment, route tasks, conserve tokens, and place the human exactly where their taste matters. Bakaus's refusal to add full auto mode is also a useful counterweight to the industry's default automation story.

My take: this is the same shape as financial advice. You do not want an unconstrained agent making irreversible money decisions from vibes; you want domain-specific skills, explicit control surfaces, and human checkpoints where judgment matters.

"There is no auto, and there will be no auto."

Why it matters: Maps neatly to Hiro's agent architecture: skills can encode financial planning, tax-aware tradeoffs, and advisor tone while keeping the human in the loop at the right level.
