Ben Thompson's Vibe-Coding Takeaway: You Still Need a Developer, Just Maybe Not a Coder

Stratechery

Thompson used Codex to build a personal home-inventory app around QR-coded storage locations, camera-based item capture, and natural-language retrieval. The interesting part is not that AI can generate app scaffolding; it is that software for tiny, idiosyncratic markets can now be worth making when the user is also the product manager.

His conclusion is bullish but usefully bounded: the coding labor collapses, but the hard parts move toward data modeling, UX judgment, deployment hygiene, and knowing when the agent is doing something technically plausible but product-hostile. My take: this is exactly the Hiro-shaped opportunity and risk. AI can make bespoke financial software feel plausible for one household at a time, but the product taste and trust boundaries matter more, not less.

"you still need a developer, just maybe not a coder."

Why it matters: A clear articulation of the new operator archetype: domain expert plus product architect plus agent wrangler. That is close to the center of gravity for AI financial advice.

AWS CEO Matt Garman Says AI Changes Entry-Level Work, But Does Not Remove the Need for Junior Talent

Platformer

Casey Newton presses AWS CEO Matt Garman on the contradiction between Amazon selling agents that recruit, code, and process claims while also hiring 11,000 interns and new grads. Garman's answer is the optimistic enterprise version of the AI labor thesis: jobs change, the middle of workflows shrinks, and the durable skill becomes learning quickly.

The strongest part of the interview is his view from AWS customers: experiments are being culled, but CIOs increasingly see a path to ROI in production workflows like claims processing and financial-services operations. My take: this is less reassuring for labor than Garman wants it to be, but very relevant for Hiro. The agent winners will be the ones that turn fuzzy productivity into traceable business value.

"'Wipe out' and 'change' are different."

Why it matters: Good strategic context for where enterprises are actually moving from demos to ROI, especially in financial-services workflows.

Bloomberg Warns Everyone Is on the Same AI Bus

Bloomberg Markets Daily

Bloomberg's market note says the AI trade is becoming both crowded and volatile, with semiconductor exposure, leveraged ETFs, and strategy correlation all reinforcing the same direction. Micron's earnings now matter less as a memory-company update and more as a stress test for the entire AI infrastructure narrative.

My take: the important signal is not 'AI bubble yes/no.' It is that capital markets are increasingly pricing AI as one factor trade. That makes every data point around memory, power, cloud capex, and model demand a potential macro event.

"Feels great on the way up, but can be extremely dangerous on the unwind."

Why it matters: Useful market temperature check: AI infrastructure optimism is now a portfolio-construction risk, not just a technology thesis.
