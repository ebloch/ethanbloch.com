Coverd's 100% Cash-Back Credit Card Wasn't Actually Live

Fintech Business Weekly

Jason Mikula's teardown of Coverd is a useful fintech pathology report: a card marketed as offering up to 100% cash back, built around gamified rewards and crypto-backed collateral, appears to have had unresolved redemption, disclosure, lending, gaming, and supervisory questions before it was promoted as launched.

The sharpest fact is operational, not rhetorical: Rain, the crypto cards-as-a-service issuer Coverd said it would use, told Fintech Business Weekly that the program had not been approved and was not live. Coverd later added a disclaimer saying the program was still under testing.

My take: this is exactly the kind of consumer-finance edge case where product language outruns the actual regulatory and operational substrate. For Hiro, the lesson is boring but important: in financial products, truthfulness is not just copy accuracy; it is alignment between marketing, program status, partner obligations, redemption mechanics, and the user's likely net impression.

"So I looked into this, and I cannot speak to any of the actual mechanics of the Coverd program, as it has not been approved and is not even a live card program"

Why it matters: Directly relevant to building financial AI products: consumer trust depends on whether the operational reality, disclosures, and marketing story all match.

The Case Against Blind Tech Optimism

Crazy Stupid Tech

Fred Vogelstein's remembrance of Om Malik doubles as a thoughtful argument about what technology coverage should be now. His claim: Wired-style optimism made sense when tech's impact was still speculative, but today's technology industry looks more like Wall Street in the 1980s, with enormous power, money, and externalities.

The most useful framing is that technology is a tool multiplier, not a moral direction. It can cure disease, create knowledge access, and improve coordination; it can also amplify lies, addiction, monopoly power, and social harm.

My take: this is a good antidote to both AI boosterism and reflexive doom. The right stance is not optimism or pessimism, but consequence-aware ambition: build powerful tools, then stay honest about their incentives and second-order effects.

"For all the talk about how Silicon Valley was revolutionizing humans and humanity, all it was really doing was supplying us with a new set of tools."

Why it matters: Useful operating philosophy for Hiro and OpenAI work: financial AI should be ambitious, but also explicit about failure modes, incentives, and misuse.
