ChatGPT Starts Plugging Into Personal Finance

TLDR AI

OpenAI is previewing a ChatGPT personal finance experience for Pro users in the US, with connected financial accounts, spending dashboards, and questions grounded in a user's actual financial context and goals. TLDR Fintech separately notes the Plaid partnership: read-only account connections that can turn generic advice into concrete guidance like which card balance to pay down first.

The important shift is not the dashboard. It is the move from conversational advice into an account-connected financial operating layer. That puts the product much closer to the moments where users need judgment, prioritization, and trust, while keeping execution outside the assistant for now.

My take: this is the correct first wedge. Read-only aggregation creates utility without crossing into regulated action too quickly; the next hard problem is whether users trust the assistant enough to let it recommend consequential choices repeatedly, not whether the charts look good.

"The feature lets users securely connect financial accounts, view spending dashboards, and ask questions grounded in their financial context and goals."

Why it matters: Directly tied to Ethan's OpenAI personal finance work: the product frontier is moving from generic education to context-aware financial guidance.

The AI Trade Is Priced Like Certainty, Not Optionality

Dan Rasmussen

Dan Rasmussen and Chris Satterthwaite argue that AI and semiconductor valuations now reflect a correlated belief system: the market is treating uncertain long-run transformation as if the winners, economics, and capex returns are already known. Their sharpest point is that the technology can be real and still be a bad investment at current prices.

They highlight the fragility of the capex cycle: Alphabet, Amazon, Microsoft, Meta, and Oracle are guiding to roughly $775 billion of 2026 capex, much of it in chips with three- to five-year useful lives. If software efficiency, new chip architectures, Chinese open models, or simple overcapacity change the path, today's owners may fund the infrastructure while later entrants capture the surplus.

My take: this is the right counterweight to AI triumphalism. The core question is not whether AI matters; it is who earns durable returns after the first several trillion dollars of infrastructure have been competed away.

"The challenge with bubbles is not that they overstate the ultimate transformative benefits of a technology but rather that they price in many of those uncertain transformative benefits as if they are a given today."

Why it matters: Useful lens for separating product conviction from market valuation, especially as AI infrastructure spend becomes the macro story behind everything else.

Drone Warfare Is Becoming a Software and Manufacturing Problem

Latent.Space

Latent.Space's interview with Yaroslav Azhnyuk is a dense look at Ukraine's drone stack: FPV drones, terminal guidance, fiber optics, thermal cameras, interceptors, simulation, and battlefield autonomy. The most important frame is that drones are software-defined platforms, so capability can improve through iteration in a way older military platforms cannot match.

The economics are brutal. Yaroslav says FPV drones now cause 70-80% of frontline casualties, cost hundreds of dollars, and can make expensive armor and artillery look mispriced. He also points out a weird AI linkage: data center demand has driven up fiber optic cable prices, affecting fiber-guided drone economics.

My take: this is what happens when consumer electronics, autonomy, and wartime feedback loops collide. The lesson generalizes beyond defense: fast iteration plus cheap hardware can invert entire institutional procurement models.

"Out of all the casualties on the front line Between 70 and 80% are done by FPV drones."

Why it matters: The same AI and hardware dynamics reshaping software products are now visible in defense: autonomy, supply chains, iteration speed, and cost-to-effect.
