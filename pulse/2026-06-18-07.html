Agentic Commerce Is Moving From Demo to Payments Rail

TLDR Fintech

Adyen launched Agentic, a modular API suite for enterprises that want to sell through conversational AI platforms without rebuilding commerce systems channel by channel. In the same batch of signals, Apple is adding AI-assisted Wallet and Apple Pay features, Stripe is powering AWS agent payments for content owners, Shopify is opening end-to-end agentic commerce APIs, and Coinbase is positioning itself as an AI-powered financial interface.

The pattern is more important than any single product: agent UX is forcing the money layer to become programmable, consented, short-lived, and auditable. That is directly in Hiro's lane. The winners probably will not be the bots that can talk about finances; they will be the systems trusted to take bounded financial actions across messy, regulated rails.

My take: payments is becoming the first serious test of agent trust. Every product here is quietly answering the same question: what does it mean for software to spend, approve, reconcile, or trade on a user's behalf without turning into a security nightmare?

"Adyen Agentic, a suite of modular APIs that enables enterprises to sell through conversational AI platforms without rebuilding their commerce systems for each new channel."

Why it matters: This is a concrete version of Hiro's thesis: financial agents need permissioning, execution rails, and user approval loops, not just better chat.

Hermes Pressures OpenClaw With Self-Writing Skills

The Information

The Information reports that Nous Research's Hermes has passed OpenClaw on new GitHub contributors over the last 30 days, according to ClawCharts. The differentiator is not a new frontier model; it is the harness. Hermes automatically writes skills after complex tool-call-heavy tasks, then reflects on those skills when idle, refining or deleting them as better workflows emerge.

That matters because it lowers both user friction and inference cost. A task may require an expensive model the first time, but once the skill exists, the system can hand future repeats to cheaper models. It is also a reminder that the lasting product moat in personal agents may be accumulated workflow memory, not raw model access.

My take: this is the agent equivalent of product-led retention. If the assistant gets meaningfully better at your personal recurring tasks every week, switching costs stop being about UI preference and start being about losing compound operational memory.

"As [Hermes] understands your workflows, it'll self-create its own skills so you don't have to keep telling it things."

Why it matters: For Hiro, financial advice workflows will repeat constantly: categorization, prep, reconciliation, tax docs, plan updates. Self-improving skills could turn those from bespoke tasks into durable personal infrastructure.

Datacenter NIMBYism Is an Economics Problem, Not Just an Influence Problem

ChinaTalk

ChinaTalk argues that blaming Chinese propaganda for U.S. datacenter resistance is mostly cope. Yes, influence ops exist, including AI slop campaigns, but the local objections are also organic: noise, water, power, aesthetics, distrust of big tech, and diffuse benefits with concentrated costs.

The proposed fix is blunt: compensate the people who bear the local burden. The piece cites a 1.6 GW datacenter that could produce around $3B in annual operator revenue and argues that paying nearby residents directly, funding schools, or making sites genuinely valuable civic assets would change zoning politics faster than blaming foreign propaganda.

My take: this is the political economy of AI capacity. Frontier capability now depends on whether hyperscalers and labs can buy not just GPUs and power, but durable local legitimacy.

"AI gains are diffuse, while the perceived pain associated with living near a datacenter is concentrated."

Why it matters: Useful frame for AI infrastructure strategy: compute buildout is no longer just capex and chips; it is permitting, community economics, and public trust.
