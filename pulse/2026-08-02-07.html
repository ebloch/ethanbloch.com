Lineage's Synapse Hangover Turns Into a New Consent Order

Fintech Business Weekly

Lineage Bank, once a small Tennessee bank that rapidly expanded through Synapse and Synctera partnerships, has been hit with a revised FDIC consent order after years of balance-sheet damage from the Synapse collapse. The new order is less punitive on capital than the 2024 version, reflecting a recapitalization that pushed the bank's leverage ratio back to roughly 15.18%, but it still demands a three-year business plan, stronger credit governance, brokered deposit reductions, and interest-rate-risk mitigation.

The signal for fintech is straightforward: partner-bank fragility is not a historical footnote. Even after Lineage exited BaaS and recapitalized, the regulatory remediation tail is long. For any financial AI or embedded-finance product, the durability of the regulated balance-sheet partner remains part of the product architecture, not a back-office vendor choice.

My take: this is exactly why bank-partnership diligence should look more like infrastructure diligence. If a fintech's core customer promise depends on a bank's capital plan, deposit mix, board competence, and third-party controls, those are product risks.

"The bank has fully transitioned to traditional community banking lines of business and away from banking as a service entirely."

Why it matters: Highly relevant to Hiro's operating model: regulated financial products need partner-risk awareness built into both strategy and user trust.

Fintechs Are Still Chasing Charters, But the Bar Is Splitting Winners From Tourists

Fintech Business Weekly

Upstart received conditional OCC approval for Upstart Bank just four months after applying, while Flex, Dakota, and Increase are each pursuing or launching bank-like structures for lending, rent payments, stablecoins, custody, and programmable money movement. Increase's move is especially notable because it turns a bank acquisition into a product platform for ACH, wires, FedNow, checks, and push-to-card.

The contrast with Wise matters. The OCC publicly rejected Wise's national trust bank application, citing AML/CFT weaknesses and insufficient fiduciary banking experience. The message is not anti-fintech; it is anti-unprepared-fintech. Strong applicants with operating history and credible governance may move quickly, while weak or incomplete filings get made examples of.

My take: the pendulum has not simply swung from restrictive to permissive. It has moved toward speed for serious operators and public discipline for applicants that cannot prove control maturity.

"A well-run charter process can be both timely and rigorous."

Why it matters: Useful context for AI finance strategy: owning more of the regulated stack can reduce dependency, but only if compliance and governance are first-class capabilities.
