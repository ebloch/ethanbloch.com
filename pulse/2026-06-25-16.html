Money Stuff: AI Lets Five-Person Hedge Funds Punch Like Desks of Fifty

Bloomberg Money Stuff

Matt Levine links the spreadsheet revolution in 1980s finance to today's AI tooling. The historical analogy is clean: spreadsheets let small buyout shops model deals at a speed previously reserved for large institutions; now LLMs let boutique macro and bond funds parse central-bank speeches, filings, inflation data, and investment-committee tone without hiring whole analyst teams.

The practical takeaway is that AI is not just a productivity layer. In finance, it changes firm formation costs and therefore market structure. The next weird winner may be a small shop that uses agents to monitor more markets, languages, and documents than its headcount should allow.

My take: this is the institutional version of the same shift Hiro is betting on for individuals. The edge is not 'AI writes a memo'; it is 'AI makes a smaller actor operationally legible and competitive.'

"AI allows a focused boutique organization like us to build powerful capabilities much earlier in our lifespan."

Why it matters: Supports the thesis that AI compresses financial expertise into smaller teams and eventually into consumer-grade advisory workflows.

Om Malik, 1966-2026

Crazy Stupid Tech

Fred Vogelstein forwarded the note from Om Malik's family: Om died June 24 at Stanford Hospital after a long heart-health journey. This is not a market story, but it belongs near the top because Om was one of the people who made tech blogging feel like an actual intellectual place: personal, networked, observant, and early to many of the shifts that now define the industry.

For Ethan, the relevance is partly human and partly editorial. Pulse itself sits downstream of the world Om helped shape: independent voices, internet-native analysis, and people treating technology as culture rather than just product news.

"Om Malik passed away on June 24, 2026, at Stanford Hospital after a long health journey with his heart."

Why it matters: A significant loss in the tech-writing world, and a reminder that taste and trusted editorial judgment still matter in an AI-mediated information diet.

The Bucks Probably Sold Giannis After the Peak

Silver Bulletin

Silver Bulletin frames the Giannis trade as portfolio-management failure: Milwaukee waited until age, injuries, contract leverage, and fewer asset-rich bidders lowered the return for a former top-three NBA asset. The Heat may still have made a rational bet because the NBA's current CBA makes true cornerstones much harder to acquire than role players.

The useful non-sports read is that teams, like investors, struggle to sell beloved assets before decline is obvious. By the time everyone agrees the window is closing, the market has usually already repriced it.

"Trading players when they can fetch the most value is optimal portfolio management — though 'selling high' is difficult for any number of reasons."

Why it matters: Good cultural context plus a clean analogy for asset timing, optionality, and emotional resistance to selling high.
