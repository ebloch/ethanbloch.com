Harness Engineering Is Becoming the Real Agent Battleground

AINews

Lilian Weng's new research recap is the strongest signal in today's stack: frontier agents are not just about larger models, but about the harness around them: goals, tools, memory, execution, evaluation, and context management. AINews frames this as part of recursive self-improvement, because the system around the model is increasingly what lets models improve workflows and successor models.

The tension is useful: OpenAI voices in The Information argue much of today's harness work may get absorbed into future models, while Weng's view is that goals and context specification will remain. My take: for Hiro, that means the defensible product work is not ornate scaffolding. It is the small set of durable financial workflows, controls, and integrations that survive as models get better.

"Even when many harness improvement[s] get eventually internalized into core model, the need to specify goals and context will not disappear."

Why it matters: Directly relevant to Hiro's product architecture: model gains matter, but trustworthy financial agents still need workflow definition, permissions, auditability, and context.

Vibe Coding Leaves the Terminal and Starts Looking Like Personal Software

Platformer

Casey Newton tries Raycast's Glaze and lands on a more interesting point than 'non-programmers can code': the next wave of AI software creation may be Mac/mobile-native, visual, and deeply personal. The examples are small but telling: a custom archive search app, a source-management CRM, and a ridiculous to-do app that makes chores fun.

The insight is that personalization becomes viable when the cost of building drops below the threshold of seriousness. That matters for consumer finance because many valuable tools are too idiosyncratic for a SaaS team to prioritize, but perfect for an agent to assemble around one person's habits.

"The new vibe-coding tools promise a world where whatever sucks about the software you use can be changed - instantly."

Why it matters: A useful reminder that consumer AI may win by letting users reshape workflows around themselves, not by shipping one static app for everyone.

The Hidden Chemistry Layer Under the AI Chip Boom

ChinaTalk

ChinaTalk's semiconductor-gases discussion is a good antidote to GPU abstraction. Helium, silane, fluorine-based etching gases, doping gases, and ultra-pure specialty chemicals are physical chokepoints beneath the AI capex story. The Strait of Hormuz example is concrete: Qatar's Ras Laffan facility produces about 15% of global helium capacity, and a disruption can hit every fab because helium is used for cooling across semiconductor manufacturing.

My take: the AI supply chain is still priced and narrated like compute is mainly chips plus power. The deeper stack includes chemistry, logistics, metrology, and geopolitical concentration that can produce non-obvious bottlenecks.

"People don't realize that semiconductors are basically made using gas."

Why it matters: Useful market context for AI infrastructure investing: the bottlenecks are not only Nvidia, TSMC, and energy.
