OpenAI Moves Inside Customers Bank's Operating Workflows

Fintech Takes

Customers Bank announced a multiyear OpenAI partnership that puts OpenAI technical teams onsite to build AI capabilities across commercial lending, deposits, payments, onboarding, portfolio monitoring, and risk tools. The operational goals are aggressive: move commercial loan closing from 30-45 days to 7 days, cut deposit account opening from a day to under 20 minutes, and push the efficiency ratio from 49% into the low 40s.

The interesting part is not the AI-clone earnings call stunt. It is that OpenAI gets supervised access to a chartered bank's real workflows, compliance constraints, vendor-risk process, and domain language. My take: this looks less like a one-off enterprise deal and more like the seed of a reusable commercial banking agent harness. If it works, Customers becomes the reference implementation for selling into other banks.

The hard question is regulatory ownership. Once vendor engineers are sitting inside bank workflows and co-developing systems that touch lending and payments, the usual third-party risk categories start to feel too blunt.

"OpenAI engineers will be operating inside Customers' lending, deposit, and payments businesses, co-developing tools the bank will run on"

Why it matters: This is directly relevant to Ethan's OpenAI personal-finance work: the deal previews how banks may let AI vendors inside regulated workflows, and where the product, compliance, and distribution leverage could appear.

AI Is Rewriting the Network Stack Behind the Hype

On my Om

Om Malik frames AI as the next stage in a long internet infrastructure continuum: internet 1.0, cloud, mobile, data, machine learning, and now AI. The consumer-facing question is whether personal agents will make home and mobile networks meaningfully faster; his answer is that the bigger changes are happening elsewhere, in physical pipes, data-center demand profiles, ownership of network capacity, and the capital intensity needed to compete.

The useful lens is that AI is not a separate technology wave floating above infrastructure. It is pulling networking, data centers, capital markets, and cloud architecture into one system. My take: for agentic products, this matters because latency and integration costs will increasingly be shaped by infrastructure ownership, not just model quality.

"AI is only supersizing everything, including the sheer scale of capital it needs to build competitive advantage."

Why it matters: Useful background for thinking about finance agents as always-on systems: the product experience will depend on the invisible network and compute economics beneath it.

GameStop's eBay Bid Shows the Limits of Meme-Stock Balance Sheets

Money Stuff

GameStop proposed buying eBay for about $56 billion in cash and stock, using roughly $8 billion of balance-sheet cash, $20 billion of debt financing against the combined company, and about $28 billion of newly issued GameStop shares. Matt Levine's core point is that the arithmetic sort of works, but the governance and capital-structure details get weird fast.

The stock portion alone would require about 1.05 billion new shares at Friday's price, while GameStop has only 1 billion authorized common shares and about 448 million already outstanding. The deal would make legacy eBay holders the economic majority of the combined company even though GameStop would be the nominal acquirer. My take: the proposal is a useful case study in how high market capitalization can become strategic currency, but only if corporate mechanics can keep up.

"GameStop is offering to buy eBay for cash it doesn't have, and also for stock it doesn't have."

Why it matters: A sharp markets-and-incentives example: financial engineering, governance rights, executive ambition, and narrative-driven equity value all collide in one transaction.
