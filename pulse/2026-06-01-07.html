AI's Consumer Surplus Is Showing Up as Small-Team Leverage

Dan Rasmussen / Verdad

Verdad frames today's AI boom less as vendor revenue and more as consumer surplus: one developer on a $100 Claude Code plan estimates he consumed roughly $15,000 of API-equivalent compute, while Stanford's Digital Economy Lab estimates $172B of AI consumer surplus against $14.2B of AI revenue. The interesting part is not the arbitrage math; it is what that cheap capacity lets a small research shop attempt.

Verdad says it is now ingesting terabytes of financial and alternative data, structuring 100,000 biotech events, parsing Japanese governance filings, mapping Korean chaebol cross-holdings, and running distributed backtests with a team that would have needed far more engineering support three years ago. My take: this is the most concrete version of the AI productivity story. The advantage is not one magic model, it is collapsing the cost of trying more ideas while keeping human judgment focused on whether the output corresponds to reality.

"The marginal cost of trying an idea has collapsed. The number of ideas I can test in a week feels an order of magnitude higher."

Why it matters: This is close to Hiro's core operating thesis: AI changes financial work when it lets a small, expert team build and test workflows that used to require a full platform organization.

SpaceX's IPO Is Forcing Markets to Rewrite Their Own Plumbing

Bloomberg Markets Daily

Bloomberg argues SpaceX's anticipated listing is so large that the market is reorganizing around it before it happens. Index providers are changing fast-entry rules, passive managers are modeling forced buying flows, ETF issuers are racing to package exposure, and retail traders are moving into murky pre-IPO vehicles.

The OpenAI/Anthropic angle is explicit: the same rule changes and speculative plumbing could shape the next wave of mega AI listings. My take: the lesson is less about SpaceX itself than about how private-market scale now bends public-market infrastructure. The IPO is becoming a market-structure event, not just a financing event.

"Elon Musk's blockbuster SpaceX IPO is so large and economically consequential that parts of the market are reorganizing around it."

Why it matters: Useful context for personal finance and investing products: client questions about access, index inclusion, and pre-IPO exposure will become mainstream, not niche.

YouTube Is Becoming a Better Talent Filter Than Hollywood

Stratechery

Ben Thompson uses the box-office success of YouTube-originated horror films like Backrooms and Obsession to argue that YouTube's selection mechanism is more demanding than Hollywood's old gates. These creators had to earn audience attention directly, iterate in public, and build proof of demand before traditional institutions showed up.

The broader point is about gatekeepers losing informational advantage. YouTube success is not a guarantee of cinematic range, but it is a brutally honest signal of audience pull. My take: this rhymes with AI product distribution. The winning products may come from people who prove workflows in public and only later get absorbed by incumbents.

"Succeeding on YouTube is a much higher bar than the gates that currently govern Hollywood."

Why it matters: A useful reminder that distribution-native creators can become institution-grade competitors once the old gatekeepers stop controlling discovery.
