The Clearing House's Tokenized Deposit Plan Is About Control, Not Reach

Fintech Takes

The Clearing House is planning a bank-owned tokenized deposit network for instant, 24/7 settlement, aimed first at large corporate treasury use cases like programmable payments, liquidity management, and cross-border flows. Alex Johnson's read is that the framing is backwards: this is less banks opening themselves to crypto and more banks trying to preserve deposit control while offering just enough tokenization to look modern.

The useful mental model is a dial between control and reach. Stablecoins maximize reach because anyone with a wallet can receive them; tokenized deposits maximize bank control because money stays inside bank-owned rails. That matters for Hiro because the next generation of financial products will be judged by whether they actually improve user reach and liquidity, not by whether incumbents can preserve the old balance-sheet perimeter with a blockchain label.

My take: this is exactly the kind of incumbent compromise that can work in enterprise treasury but rarely creates the consumer or SMB breakthrough. It is a better RTP-plus than a real stablecoin answer.

"You cannot add reach without subtracting control"

Why it matters: Hiro will live in the same distribution fight: better financial UX often requires crossing incumbent boundaries, while incumbents will package boundary preservation as innovation.

S&P Keeps the Door Closed to Fast-Track AI IPOs

Bloomberg Money Stuff

Matt Levine connects the AI-maximalist investment thesis to a very practical index question: if SpaceX, OpenAI, and Anthropic become giant public companies but remain outside the S&P 500 because of seasoning, profitability, and float rules, passive investors are making an implicit bet against the AI boom. S&P decided not to waive those rules, unlike more flexible rivals.

The result is a weird inversion: the index that is supposed to represent the market may deliberately lag the market's most culturally and economically important new companies. That protects investors from forced exposure to expensive AI listings, but it also means passive portfolios could miss a chunk of the upside if the maximalist story turns out to be right.

My take: this is less a technical index-policy story than a governance story about who decides what ordinary investors are allowed to own by default.

"Excluding the AI portion of the market is maybe a stronger bet than you want to make."

Why it matters: Useful for Ethan's markets lens: AI distribution is now showing up in passive portfolio construction, not just venture rounds and product launches.
