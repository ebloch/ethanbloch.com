Scott Alexander Puts Numbers on the AI Endgame

Astral Codex Ten

Scott Alexander finally consolidates his AI worldview into one probability map: AGI as 90% knowledge-work automation has a 50% chance by 2034, the diffusion gap has a 50% chance of being under 10 years, and the median superhuman gap is shorter than the median diffusion gap. The important move is separating capability arrival from institutional adoption, then arguing that superhuman intelligence may arrive before ordinary enterprises finish deploying human-range AI.

His safety view is less maximalist than the hardest doomers but still severe: about 20% p(doom) given current corporate safety effort, with a real chance that automated alignment researchers arrive early enough to help. My take: the most useful part for Hiro is the diffusion framing. Financial advice is exactly the kind of domain where the model can be capable before the world trusts, integrates, regulates, and operationalizes it.

"Since my median superhuman gap is shorter than my median diffusion gap, in most timelines I predict we have superhuman intelligence before human-range intelligence has finished diffusing."

Why it matters: This is directly relevant to Hiro's strategy: the bottleneck may not be model intelligence, but trust, integration, compliance, and customer adoption.

SpaceX IPO Pricing Looks Like the Private Market Already Did the Work

Bloomberg Money Stuff

Matt Levine's SpaceX piece is really about the changing boundary between private and public markets. SpaceX set a fixed $135 IPO price, secondary markets were already around that level, and pre-IPO perpetual futures plus prediction markets implied roughly a 20% first-day pop. The IPO is still the real test, but the old idea that public trading suddenly discovers the price looks weaker when private stock markets and synthetic betting markets are already liquid enough to anchor expectations.

My take: this matters beyond SpaceX. If late-stage private company prices become observable and bettable, wealth management clients will increasingly expect public-market style transparency and advice around assets that are still technically private.

"Possibly the giant private companies can go public without much drama, because their shares already trade, and because the modern merger of financial markets with betting markets creates efficient price discovery."

Why it matters: Useful for thinking about private-company exposure, client expectations, and how prediction/secondary markets may bleed into personal finance products.
