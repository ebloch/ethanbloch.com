Who Gets to Profit From Someone Else's Story?

Bloomberg On Books

Bloomberg's lead item uses Josh Silver's buzzy novel Fruit Fly to reopen the uncomfortable question of who gets to tell, package, and monetize stories rooted in identity, trauma, or marginalization. The book's setup is deliberately queasy: a blocked, affluent novelist befriends a young gay addict under false pretenses, then turns his life into her own material.

The useful angle for Ethan is not literary gossip; it is consent and representation as product design. AI products will increasingly intermediate personal narratives, financial anxieties, and private context. The hard part is not whether the system can summarize someone's life. It is whether the person whose life is being transformed into value has agency in the transaction.

My take: this maps directly onto consumer AI finance. Trust will come less from clever personalization and more from making provenance, permission, and user benefit legible.

"Who gets to tell whose story?"

Why it matters: Hiro will handle intimate user context. This is a sharp reminder that personalization without explicit user agency can feel extractive.

A Former Googler Turns Corporate Mythmaking Into Evidence

Bloomberg On Books

Mark Bergen highlights Claire Stapleton's Don't Be Evil, a memoir from a former Google executive ghostwriter who helped produce the company's internal mythology before becoming a Google Walkout organizer. The book's power, as summarized here, is that it treats corporate language as evidence: slogans, all-hands narratives, and executive theater become the machinery by which a company explains itself to employees.

The most telling anecdote is almost absurdist: a colleague's notes from a Larry Page meeting read, 'Bladerunner + US govt = Google?' That is funny, but also useful. Big tech culture often hides strategic ambiguity inside inspirational language until the ambiguity turns into politics.

My take: as AI companies become infrastructure, internal story discipline matters. The gap between what a company says it is and how power actually works eventually becomes product risk.

"Whose version of the story would people believe?"

Why it matters: Relevant to OpenAI and Hiro alike: mission language only compounds trust if the operating reality supports it.
