Bank-Fintech Risk Standards May Become the New Compliance Theater

Fintech Business Weekly

The FDIC is working with major bank and fintech trade groups on a voluntary standards body for third-party risk management, tentatively centered on BISDO and a RAMP certification registry. The goal is sensible: let banks and fintech service providers reuse a common diligence and assessment layer instead of repeating bespoke questionnaires across every partnership.

The weakness is just as clear. The draft framework is voluntary, gives banks no safe harbor, does not blacklist uncertified providers, and explicitly keeps bank responsibility intact. That may help the effort move fast and attract broad industry buy-in, but it also risks producing a badge that looks like risk control without doing the hard work of risk transfer, operational diligence, or supervisory accountability.

My take: this is directly relevant to Hiro because embedded finance and AI advice both run into the same perimeter problem. Standards are useful only if they encode specific operating evidence. Otherwise, they become procurement language with a public registry.

"If there is insufficient specificity and no enforcement mechanism, such standards may end up being little more than “compliance-washing,” giving the illusion of safety and soundness without any actual substance."

Why it matters: For an AI financial advisor, trust infrastructure matters as much as product intelligence. Any partnership, custody, data, or advice stack will be judged by how auditable and governable it is, not just whether it has a certification label.

Doomberg: The Energy Panic Is About Refining, Not Crude

Doomberg

Doomberg argues that several popular energy-market fears are analytically backward: the U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve is being used through oil exchanges that should return more barrels over time, U.S. crude is not physically scarce, and the real tightness is in refined products because global refining capacity is strained.

The most useful frame is the input-versus-bottleneck distinction. WTI can fall while diesel stays high if refineries are already running near full utilization. In that world, crude is not the scarce asset; operable refining capacity is. Doomberg also argues that North America remains unusually strong because U.S. production, Canadian heavy crude, and Gulf Coast refining create a self-sufficient regional energy base.

My take: the broader lesson travels well beyond energy. In complex systems, people often obsess over the visible inventory number while the binding constraint sits elsewhere in the conversion chain.

"When the only customer of an input is running full throttle, and the price of that input is falling precipitously, there is clearly no physical shortage."

Why it matters: Useful market mental model: look for the bottleneck, not the loudest commodity price. That habit matters in finance, AI infrastructure, and product operations.
