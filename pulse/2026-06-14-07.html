Debanking Is Becoming the New Battlefield for Bank Regulation

Fintech Business Weekly

Jason Mikula frames DOJ subpoenas to JPMorgan, Bank of America, and other banks as one visible piece of a much larger campaign to reshape bank supervision. The important move is not just investigating alleged debanking; it is the broader effort to remove reputational risk from supervisory practice, legitimize crypto access to banking rails, and centralize more control over agencies that used to have more independent posture.

For Hiro, the strategic point is that banking access and compliance categories are becoming more politically contingent. If the regulatory pendulum starts swinging harder between administrations, fintech products that depend on bank partnerships, payments access, or crypto-adjacent policy need more explicit scenario planning.

My take: this is a reminder that financial infrastructure is not neutral plumbing. The rulebook is being rewritten around ideology, industry lobbying, and personal interests, and the winners will be the companies that can adapt without making their customer promise depend on one regulatory mood.

"Regulation of the U.S. financial system has always been political, even if, historically, it was less explicitly so."

Why it matters: Hiro will live inside bank, broker, and payments infrastructure. This is the kind of policy volatility that can turn a clean product thesis into an operational constraint.

Egypt's New Capital Is a Parable About Building Too Much and Building Badly

ChinaTalk

ChinaTalk's travel essay on Egypt's New Administrative Capital turns into a sharp comparison between authoritarian building capacity and liberal-democratic paralysis. Egypt can throw up ministries, towers, a massive park, surveillance-ready roads, and millions of housing units in the desert, yet still produce a place that looks more useful to aerial photography and regime control than to ordinary life.

The China angle is that Chinese state construction capacity is being exported as a full stack: finance, buildings, smart-city infrastructure, and surveillance-ready urban form. The host country bears much of the livability and debt risk; Chinese SOEs get paid to build.

My take: this belongs in Pulse because it is really about institutional product design. Capacity without feedback loops makes monuments; process without capacity makes shortages. The hard problem is finding the governance layer that can build quickly while still letting reality correct the plan.

"One model can build a city for six million people and leave it empty. The other struggles to build enough housing for the people already there."

Why it matters: This connects Ethan's interest in abundance, cities, and China to a broader operating question: what kinds of institutions can actually ship useful things at scale?
