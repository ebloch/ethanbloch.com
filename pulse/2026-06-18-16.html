AMP's Bet: Make FLOPs Flow Like Megawatts

Latent.Space

Latent.Space's interview with Anjney Midha frames the next AI infrastructure fight less as a GPU procurement contest and more as a utilization, scheduling, and incentive-alignment problem. The core claim: frontier labs can spend enormous capex and still waste the useful work if clusters are poorly scheduled, badly integrated, or misaligned across owners, operators, and researchers.

AMP's proposed answer is a neutral compute grid: pool demand, pool supply, make base load predictable, and let labs spike usage dynamically. That matters because the physical-world constraints around power, permitting, and community backlash are starting to look as important as model architecture.

My take: this is the grown-up version of the AI infra conversation. The industry talks as if the constraint is only chips; the more useful question is how much intelligence per watt, per permit, and per dollar actually reaches users.

"The goal is to try to make FLOPs flow like megawatts."

Why it matters: Hiro is an agent product, so this is relevant twice: better infra economics shape model costs, and the grid metaphor is a useful way to think about agent capacity, reliability, and routing.

The SpaceX IPO Pop Became an ETF Arbitrage Trade

Bloomberg Money Stuff

Matt Levine walks through a synthetic way to capture the hot-IPO pop without getting an IPO allocation: pour capital into an ETF expected to receive SpaceX shares, hedge the rest of the ETF basket, then redeem after the IPO move. ARKK saw a record $4.6 billion inflow followed by a $6.2 billion outflow, coinciding with its SpaceX allocation.

The cleverness is also the problem. Existing fund holders can be diluted if fast money uses the creation-redemption mechanism to extract the IPO benefit that long-term holders thought they owned.

My take: this is a clean example of financial product plumbing creating a shadow market around scarce access. The more private-market exposure gets packaged into liquid wrappers, the more these NAV-timing and allocation games matter.

"Investors appear to be using ARKK as a piggyback vehicle for access to opportunities they may not be able to easily buy directly."

Why it matters: Useful for Hiro because it shows how retail-facing products can hide complex access, allocation, and fairness dynamics behind a simple ticker.

Anthropic's Coding Edge as Prepared-Mind Luck

Latent.Space

The sharpest company-building section in the Midha interview is his explanation of Anthropic's coding success. He rejects the idea that Claude's coding leap was merely a lucky model roll; his view is that Anthropic had spent years building the culture, paranoia, efficiency, and focus that let it compound the right signal when coding became the decisive capability.

That connects to a broader point: too much capital too early can make labs fragile because they never have to define the one thing that matters most. Anthropic's P0, in his telling, was coding because coding could accelerate all computer work and therefore accelerate AGI.

My take: the lesson generalizes. Scarcity clarifies the product truth; abundance often lets teams postpone it.

"Anthropic has been the most prepared company for four years."

Why it matters: For Hiro, the useful question is not just what the product can do, but what scarce-resource P0 forces the team to become excellent at.
