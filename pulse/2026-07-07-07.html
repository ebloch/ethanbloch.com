Ben Thompson's Script for Meta: Stop Selling Platform Dreams, Sell the AI Feed

Stratechery

Thompson writes a fictional Zuckerberg earnings-call script that reframes Meta's AI spending around the company's actual strength: ranking, distribution, and monetizing human attention. The provocative move is the mea culpa: Meta's big strategic mistakes came from wanting to own a platform, while its great business came from being the best app for a changing media substrate.

The AI argument is that Meta does not need to win AGI or own the operating system. It needs enough model capability, enough infrastructure, and enough product instinct to make feeds, ads, creators, companions, and content generation work better inside apps with billions of users.

My take: this is the cleaner public-market version of the AI product question. The winning consumer AI company may not be the one with the most elegant model story; it may be the one that can turn model capability into habit, distribution, and revenue without asking users to understand the stack.

"I know that Meta and myself are facing a lot of questions about AI, particularly the amount of money we are spending on capex."

Why it matters: Useful for Hiro because it separates model capability from product power. The moat is not just intelligence; it is workflow ownership, trust, distribution, and the ability to monetize repeated use.

Fable Is Becoming a New Kind of Thinking Harness

ben's bites

Ben's Bites is less interested in Fable as a coding tool and more interested in it as a sparring partner: resourceful, creative, long-running, and able to keep a thread of intent alive across messy exploration. AINews echoes the same pattern from user reports: Fable's edge is not always local code quality, but project-level judgment, context persistence, and avoiding dead-end implementation paths.

That distinction matters. Coding agents are becoming general workbench environments, but their product shape still defaults to developer execution. The unmet demand is a harness that can build when needed while behaving more like a creative chief of staff: exploratory, skeptical, memory-rich, and comfortable with ambiguous work.

My take: this is the frontier-product gap hiding in plain sight. Users do not just want stronger models; they want interaction shells tuned to kinds of thinking. Finance, planning, writing, and strategy each need their own harness norms.

"I'm wanting a harness that can still build but is less of a developer at heart, and more something to really spar with me on creative thinking."

Why it matters: Directly relevant to Hiro's assistant design. A financial advisor agent should not feel like a chatbot bolted onto a dashboard; it needs the right harness for deliberation, planning, memory, and action.

AI Infrastructure Is Running Into Molecules, Not Just GPUs

Doomberg

Doomberg's Project Kilby piece is a useful antidote to abstract AI infrastructure talk. Chevron, Microsoft, GE Vernova, and Caterpillar are backing a West Texas gas-to-data project that could consume roughly 2.7 GW of electricity and, by Doomberg's estimate, about 1 bcf/day of natural gas.

The important point is not just that AI needs power. It is that the power bottleneck changes commodity flows, Permian gas economics, turbine supply, permitting politics, and oil drilling economics. If a blue-chip, behind-the-meter project near stranded gas struggles to reach FID, the rest of the data-center buildout gets harder to underwrite.

My take: AI capex is now an energy, permitting, and industrial supply-chain story. The software market will price model progress daily, but the real constraint may be how fast physical systems can absorb demand.

"Trillions of dollars in market value depend on the answer."

Why it matters: Relevant for AI strategy and markets: the agent economy depends on physical infrastructure whose timelines, politics, and commodity exposures look nothing like software deployment.
