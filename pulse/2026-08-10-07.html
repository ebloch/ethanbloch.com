Computer-use agents are crossing from demo to operations

a16z

a16z's field report says computer-using agents are now viable in narrow, repeatable production workflows: updating records, clicking through legacy portals, processing IT tickets, and acting as a self-healing fallback when brittle scrapers break. The important shift is that the execution layer is becoming less special; the moat moves to workflow context, permissions, verification, escalation, and the ability to prove ROI.

For Hiro, this is a useful framing: the model is only one component of an operational system. The durable product work is deciding what the agent is allowed to do, how it knows it succeeded, when it escalates, and how the company-specific context is captured without turning every deployment into services work.

My take: this is the same pattern as coding agents. Capability gets attention first, but trust and verification decide adoption.

"when your heaviest users stop checking the leaderboard, the leaderboard has stopped being the story."

Why it matters: Hiro's advisor thesis lives or dies on reliable workflows, not just smarter chat. This is a clean map of where enterprise agents are actually useful today.

Apple's growth is now constrained by AI-era chip scarcity

Stratechery

Ben Thompson reads Apple's weak September guidance as a supply story, not a demand story: iPhone and Mac sales were strong, but TSMC advanced-node capacity is tighter because AI accelerators and custom inference chips are competing for the same leading-edge process. Apple may be insulated from the AI product race, but not from the AI supply chain.

The Amazon side is the mirror image: Andy Jassy argues AI pulls more non-AI compute into AWS because post-training reinforcement learning, agent tool use, storage, vector databases, and orchestration all sit near existing cloud workloads. In other words, the AI capex boom does not just sell GPUs; it reshapes demand for CPUs and cloud adjacency.

My take: the second-order infrastructure effects are becoming the real market story. AI is no longer a sector theme; it is a capacity allocator.

"the primary issue is advanced nodes that we run our SOCs on."

Why it matters: Useful market lens: AI capex is creating winners and bottlenecks outside obvious model labs, including Apple and AWS.

High yield may be missing this credit cycle's riskiest lending

Dan Rasmussen

Verdad argues the high-yield market has matured into a less leveraged, shorter-duration, less speculative asset class. The SaaS and AI lending binge mostly went into private credit, leaving public junk bonds with relatively little software exposure and limited direct exposure to BDCs or life insurers.

That does not make high yield risk-free; spreads can still widen in a macro shock. But it suggests the traditional high-yield spread may be a weaker signal for where credit excess is building, because the mess has moved into less transparent private markets.

My take: this is the market equivalent of looking in the wrong log file. The public signal may look calmer because the riskiest traffic was routed elsewhere.

"This might be the first speculative lending bubble since the 1980s that doesn’t hit the junk market."

Why it matters: Relevant to portfolio thinking and fintech risk: private credit may be carrying the hidden AI/SaaS credit exposure public markets used to reveal.
