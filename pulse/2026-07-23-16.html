Ethan Mollick's Practical Map for Which AI to Use Now

One Useful Thing

Mollick's core argument is that 'using AI' now means much more than chatting with a model: the real productivity jump comes from agentic systems that can use tools, access files, and operate a computer. He frames ChatGPT and Claude as the two practical defaults for real work, with the important split between cloud-hosted agent modes and local-computer agents like Codex and Claude Code.

The useful bit for Hiro is the permissions model. Agents become valuable exactly when they can touch real systems, but that is also where prompt injection, accidental sends, and over-broad access matter. His advice is blunt: keep approval gates on for anything that sends, spends, changes files, or deletes.

My take: this is a mainstream user-education piece for the same product problem Hiro faces. The hard part is not making an agent smart; it is making scoped authority understandable enough that normal people will trust it with consequential work.

"working with these systems is more like managing than it is chatting"

Why it matters: Directly relevant to Hiro's agent UX: delegation, tool access, approval boundaries, and user trust are becoming the product surface.

Sports Performance Indexes Are the Latest Financial Wrapper

Money Stuff

Matt Levine's lead item is funny because it starts as a joke about prediction-market indexes and ends as a real product category: sports statistics turned into rules-based indexes that can support futures, ETFs, and swaps. The stated use case is hedging sports-industry risks, but the more plausible business is packaging fan attention into tradeable exposure.

The structural insight is broader than sports. Financial markets keep finding messy domains, converting binary events into continuous numbers, and then selling the wrapper as liquidity, hedging, or uncorrelated returns.

My take: this is the fintech pattern to watch. The product story is risk management; the consumer behavior is speculation with a more respectable interface.

"Turning a series of binary sports bets into a single continuous number is good for selling financial products."

Why it matters: Useful lens for Hiro: customers often meet finance through wrappers. The job is to explain what exposure they actually own, not just the story on the box.
