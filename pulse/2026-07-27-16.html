Lead Stories

Tyler Cowen argues AI writing will become something readers want

The Free Press

Cowen reacts to Substack's new AI-writing detector by taking the opposite side of the reader-panic trade: today's AI prose is often irritating, but the rational response is to want better AI writing, not a permanent human-only label.

The useful bit is not the detector itself. It is the way publishing markets may split between provenance as a premium signal and AI fluency as a baseline production skill. If AI-written work gets good enough, the scarce asset shifts from typing to taste, trust, and editorial judgment.

My take: this is directly relevant to consumer AI products. People do not actually want anti-AI purity; they want output that feels intelligent, accountable, and worth their time.

"I want AI writing that can fool me, and maybe sometimes I am already getting it."

Why it matters: For Hiro, the lesson is that disclosure matters less than trust and usefulness. The product has to earn the right to sound fluent by being grounded, specific, and obviously on the user's side.
